<Section id="the-ratio" tone="slate" title="The Ratio" intro="Concessional capital is any money that accepts less than it could earn elsewhere: grants, guarantees, below-market loans. It is scarce, so an intervention that uses it should be judged by how much durable impact each dollar buys." wide>

<Ratio />

The size of the change comes from the [levers](/framework/mechanisms/) an intervention uses. The duration comes from the [diagnosis](/framework/): whether the gap is transitional or structural, and how long the lever's effect or the payer lasts. The value per unit depends on [who buys](/framework/payers/) and what they do with what they save. Most claims about market interventions are weakest on probability, contribution and expected cost.

The result uses the same units as direct programs, so that market interventions compete with direct delivery on equal terms. GiveWell, an evaluator that directs donations to the charities it finds most cost-effective, measures good done in <Term>units of value</Term>, where doubling one person's consumption for a year counts as one. It compares programs as multiples of a benchmark of about 0.003 units per dollar. Until November 2025 it called these multiples of cash. The worked example and the retrospective below use those multiples.

</Section>

<Section id="why-durability-multiplies-impact" title="Why Durability Multiplies Impact">

<Fig takeaway="Impact is the area between the outcome with and without the intervention." caption="In a transitional gap, spending stops early while impact continues until the market would have reached the same outcome on its own. In a structural gap, impact lasts as long as the payer keeps paying.">
  <ImpactArea />
  <div class="legend" slot="legend"><span><i class="k-soc"></i>outcome with intervention</span><span><i class="k-cf"></i>counterfactual</span><span><i class="k-wedge"></i>funder spending</span></div>
</Fig>

Market interventions get their leverage in transitional gaps because the impact continues after spending stops, though it counts only until the market would have reached the same outcome anyway. Impact after that point lacks <Term>additionality</Term>, so a transitional intervention should claim only the acceleration it caused, plus any permanent difference. The long debate over the pneumococcal advance market commitment is mainly about what would have happened without it ([see the case](/cases/pneumococcal-amc/)).

In a structural gap, impact lasts only as long as the payer keeps paying. Setup costs are amortized over the payer's lifetime, and a payer that quits early strands them. Political and budgetary durability is therefore a cost-effectiveness parameter, to be estimated with the other terms.

</Section>

<Section id="the-terms" tone="slate" title="The Terms">

### Size of the Change

Price here means what the <Term t="Buyer">buyer</Term> spends per unit of the outcome it values, such as a patient-year of viral suppression, a delivered test result or a ton of carbon removed. Pricing per outcome lets a product switch, a bundled service contract and a unit-price cut be compared directly. [Mechanisms](/framework/mechanisms/) shows how each lever changes it.

### Duration Beyond the Counterfactual

Duration is usually the largest term in the ratio and the one analyses model least. It depends on whether the lever's effect lasts after the funder exits and on when the outcome would have arrived anyway, through generic entry after patent expiry, a competitor's product, falling costs elsewhere or another funder acting. That date is best modeled as explicit scenarios drawn from the product's own price trend before the intervention, the dates competitors were due to arrive, and how prices moved in comparable markets when they did.

<div class="callout callout-amber"><span class="label">Common error</span>Claiming credit for all the volume that follows a price drop. Credit runs only until the market would have reached the same price without the intervention, plus any permanent difference.</div>

### Probability of Success

Deals can fail through missed deliveries, registration delays or optimistic forecasts, so the outcome should be weighted by an explicit probability. A called guarantee changes the cost-effectiveness of the intervention in retrospect and raises the question of whether the gap or market failure was misdiagnosed.

### Value per Unit

A lower price frees buyer dollars, whose value depends on where the money goes and what each use is worth, and the two are best estimated separately. Where a government or donor buys on others' behalf, part of the money usually buys more of the same product and the rest returns to the buyer's wider budget. A dollar spent on more of the product is valued by what the extra product achieves, and one returned to the budget by whatever the buyer funds next, which is rarely nothing ([what a dollar saved is worth](/framework/payers/#value-of-savings)). Where price itself was the barrier to access, the value is the additional people reached.

For most market interventions, converting freed dollars into value takes the most work. A ratio of buyer dollars per funder dollar can describe an exceptional grant or a weak one, depending on what a marginal dollar of the Global Fund or a ministry of health buys. GiveWell's own working figures vary widely by buyer. [Its staff have estimated](https://blog.givewell.org/2024/06/21/the-fungibility-question/) that Global Fund spending displaced by its grants is worth about 0.015 units of value per dollar, roughly four and a half times its benchmark, and displaced Gavi spending about 0.007, roughly twice.

<div class="callout callout-amber"><span class="label">Common error</span>Stopping at dollars saved, or counting only the share redeployed. Each saved dollar is worth its next use, including the part not spent on the product.</div>

### Counterfactual Contribution

Most market interventions involve several funders, a catalyst and the buyer, so a funding decision rests on what this funder's participation changes, that is, the outcome with it minus the outcome without it.

The contribution is best estimated by scenario, with each weighted by its likelihood. If the deal would have collapsed without this funder, its contribution is the whole change, less the value of what co-funders' money would have done had they spent it elsewhere. If another funder would have stepped in, the deal happens either way, and the contribution shrinks to the value of the money that funder keeps for other uses. The weighted result can be far above or far below an even split. GiveWell builds the same logic into its estimates as explicit <Term>leverage and funging</Term> adjustments.

Dividing credit among partners, by share of cost or by agreement, is for reporting. It stops the field from double counting in its reports, because counterfactual contributions need not add up to the whole: two partners who were each indispensable each caused nearly all of the change. Credit shares should not be used to decide whether to fund.

<div class="callout callout-amber"><span class="label">Common error</span>Using a credit share as the contribution term. Dividing the result among the partners looks conservative but answers a different question, and it can understate or overstate what one funder's money changed.</div>

### Expected Cost

The relevant cost is the funder's expected spending, which for a large guarantee that is rarely called may be a small fraction of the commitment. It includes the cost of capital held against contingent commitments and of the <Term>catalyst</Term> whose work makes the instrument function.

</Section>

<Section id="worked-example" title="A Worked Example" intro="The figures below are hypothetical and describe no real deal. They show how the terms combine and how much the common errors can inflate a claim.">

A funder offers two generic manufacturers a three-year volume guarantee with a face value of $20 million, in exchange for a ceiling price on a treatment that national programs, financed partly by donors, buy for one million patients a year. The ceiling lowers the price by $30 per patient-year. Without the deal, generic competition would have brought the price down by the same amount three years later. A second foundation spends $2 million helping countries switch to the new supply, and the funder judges there is a 30 percent chance that another guarantor would have stepped in had it stayed out.

Value is stated in benchmark dollars: the amount that, spent at the benchmark's cost-effectiveness, would do the same good. The ratio is then a multiple of the benchmark.

The two columns describe the same deal at the same expected cost, a quarter of the $20 million headline commitment. The adjusted estimate is about a seventh of the headline claim, a difference that comes entirely from the numerator, with the duration term alone accounting for more than a factor of three.

The estimate is most sensitive to the terms that are hardest to observe. If generic competitors would have entered after one year instead of three, the ratio falls to about 6x the benchmark, and if they would have entered after seven years, the ratio rises to about 40x. If the buyers' marginal treatment dollar were worth half the benchmark instead of twice it, the ratio would also fall to about 6x, and at four times the benchmark it would rise to about 32x. If another guarantor would certainly have stepped in, the contribution falls to $10 million and the ratio to 2x. A range is more informative than a single figure.

In a structural gap, the duration term becomes the payer's expected lifetime and the expected cost includes every year of payment. The impact then depends on how long the payer continues.

<Section id="retrospective" tone="slate" title="A Retrospective: Contraceptive Implants" intro="The same terms applied with public data to a completed deal, the 2013 volume guarantees that halved the price of contraceptive implants.">

The usual claim for the implant guarantees is that they saved more than $500 million against the old price. A counterfactual reading starts from the price that would have prevailed without the deal. Prices were already falling before 2013, and a cheaper competitor was prequalified by the World Health Organization in 2017. Depending on how fast prices would have fallen, the deal's advantage lasted four to eight years and was worth $175 million to $400 million at the prices buyers would otherwise have paid.

What that advantage was worth depends on what buyers did with it. Buyers appear to have bought more implants and also returned some of the savings to their budgets. By 2015 volumes were well above what the 2012 budget would have bought at the old price, and spending stayed below its 2012 level until 2020. With extra implants valued at GiveWell's estimate for a year of contraception, and released money at roughly twice the benchmark, the central estimate is about 3.4 million units of value, the same good as about $1 billion spent at the benchmark.

No public source reports a payout on the guarantees. On the case study's judgments, unless the partners' combined cost exceeded about $60 million, the deal met GiveWell's current funding bar of 6x the benchmark under every counterfactual considered, and at a cost of $25 million the central estimate is about 40x. The [case study](/cases/implants/#a-retrospective-estimate) sets out the data, the judgments and the full range.

</Section>

<Section id="what-misdiagnosis-costs" tone="slate" title="What Misdiagnosis Costs">

A **bridge to nowhere**, a structural gap treated as transitional, pays for a temporary intervention whose impact ends when funding stops, so the numerator stays small. A **windfall**, a transitional gap treated as structural, keeps paying after the market could have sustained the outcome without help, so the denominator grows with no impact added. Either error lowers the ratio, so diagnosis should come before funding, and the [diagnostic](/diagnostic/) walks through it.

</Section>
