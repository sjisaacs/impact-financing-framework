## The Trap

Before 2013, Bayer's Jadelle sold for about $18 per unit and Merck's Implanon for between $16.50 and $18, depending on volume thresholds. The Clinton Health Access Initiative described the market as a high-price, low-volume trap. Suppliers kept unit prices high because orders were small and unpredictable, and orders stayed small because prices were high. As a result, donors and ministries found implants hard to budget for.

## The Intervention

In 2013, a partnership including the Gates Foundation guaranteed a minimum volume of purchases. Bayer cut Jadelle's price from $18 to $8.50 per unit, effective January 1, 2013, in more than 50 countries, under an agreement intended to reach more than 27 million women over six years. Merck reduced Implanon to the same price.

## Lever Accounting

The price drop is usually attributed to "the volume guarantee," but a breakdown by <Term>lever</Term> shows several mechanisms with different durability.

- **Scale and learning.** Multiyear volume commitments let both manufacturers invest in capacity and run at higher utilization. The economies of scale last as long as volume stays up at the new price, while what the manufacturers learned from producing more largely persists regardless.
- **Risk transfer.** The guarantee removed the demand uncertainty that had been priced into earlier offers, a saving that lasts if demand proves real, so that the guarantee is not drawn on.
- **Process improvement.** Harmonizing packaging specifications that had been fragmented across buyers increased throughput on packaging lines. The gain lasts almost regardless of what happens next.
- **Competition, delayed.** Shanghai Dahua's implant, distributed as Levoplant, was prequalified by the World Health Organization in June 2017 and offered at $6.90 per unit in 2018. Competition lasts only as long as entrants remain viable at the lower price.

## Analysis

**Diagnosis.** Donors and governments were already buying implants for women who could not pay full price, so demand existed. The barrier was the high-price, low-volume trap, a transitional gap that the guarantee navigated, after which the buyers kept buying at the lower price.

**Durability.** Each lever's gain lasts under the conditions given above. The gain from risk transfer did last, because demand proved real: both suppliers delivered more than the agreed volumes in the first year, and no public source reports a payout on the guarantees.

**Value per unit.** Because donors and governments were the buyers, most of the benefit of the price cut went to their budgets. Some of it bought more implants and some was released for other uses, and the two are worth very different amounts. The retrospective below values both.

**The counterfactual.** A lower-cost implant was already moving toward prequalification, so some price decline was likely coming. The <Term>volume guarantee</Term> can be credited only with the acceleration it caused, meaning years of lower prices and higher volume earlier than otherwise, plus any permanent difference.

**Exposure.** The low price depends partly on volume financed by donors and governments. If that volume falls, economies of scale are the part most likely to unwind, and competition may weaken if lower volume squeezes a supplier out. The process improvements and the learning should largely survive.

## A Retrospective Estimate

This section estimates what the deal was worth in the terms a funder would use to compare it with direct programs, working through the [ratio](/cost-effectiveness/) with public data. Where public data are lacking, it says so and states a judgment. It values the deal as a whole, since the public record cannot settle any partner's <Term>counterfactual contribution</Term>, the difference its participation made. Results are in GiveWell's <Term>units of value</Term> and as multiples of its benchmark, taken as 0.0033 units per dollar, the figure in its recent grant pages (its methods page rounds it to about 0.003).

### The Price Without the Deal

Prices were already falling before 2013. Jadelle cost $24 in 2008 and 2009, $22 in 2010, $19 in 2011 and $18 in 2012, a decline of 7 to 9 percent a year. Implanon fell from $20 in 2010 to $16.50 before its cut. Over the longer run from 2005 to 2011, implant prices fell only about 20 percent, or roughly 4 percent a year.

A cheaper competitor already existed. Sino-implant (II) had sold for $8 since 2008, but it lacked World Health Organization prequalification, which most donor procurement requires. It was prequalified as Levoplant on June 30, 2017, and offered at $6.90 in 2018. In comparable markets, a new supplier has lowered prices only modestly: in developing-country markets for children's antiretrovirals between 2004 and 2012, each additional manufacturer was associated with price declines of about 2 percent for originator products and 8 percent for generics.

That evidence supports three counterfactual price paths, each starting from $18 in 2012:

- **Fast.** Prices keep falling 9 percent a year through 2016, then drop to $8.50 in 2017 as the prequalified competitor arrives. The deal's advantage lasts four years.
- **Central.** Prices fall 8 percent a year through 2017, then drop to $8.50 in 2018, once buyers can choose Levoplant. The advantage lasts five years.
- **Slow.** Prices fall at the long-run 4 percent a year through 2017, then converge on $8.50 by 2021 as buyers shift to Levoplant gradually, in line with the modest response to entry in comparable markets. The advantage lasts eight years.

Over those windows, the price advantage on the implants actually bought came to about $175 million, $215 million and $400 million. The figure usually reported, more than $500 million in savings, compares implants bought at $8.50 with the old price and has no counterfactual.

Shipments were already rising before the deal, from 3.1 million in 2011 to 5.8 million in 2012 at about $18, and the partners also paid for training to expand services, so some later growth would have come without the lower price.

Public data do not show how buyers used the first year's gain. In the shipment series, spending halved in 2013 while volume rose 5 percent. Donors' procurement records, compiled by UNFPA, show the opposite: 7.5 million implants bought in 2013 for $74.4 million, against 4 million for $75.8 million in 2012. The two series differ in coverage and timing (shipments lag orders), which may explain part of the gap. Using shipments credits the deal with fewer extra implants in 2013.

By 2015, volumes were well above what the 2012 budget would have bought at the old price, while spending stayed below its 2012 level until 2020, consistent with buyers taking the lower price partly as more implants and partly as money for other uses.

What buyers would have spent without the deal cannot be observed, so the estimate brackets it:

- **Spending rises.** Buyers would have raised budgets enough to buy the same implants at the higher price, so the whole advantage is money released for other uses.
- **Spending holds.** Buyers would have kept spending up to the 2012 level of about $104 million a year: enough to buy the implants they actually bought where that sum allowed, and fewer where it did not. Implants beyond what the budget would have bought at the counterfactual price are extra implants the deal caused, and the rest of the advantage is released money. This case credits the deal with the most extra implants.
- **Midway.** Spending halfway between the two, used as the central case.

### What It Was Worth

**An extra implant.** Standard conversion factors credit an implant with 2.5 couple-years of protection for a three-year product and 3.8 for a five-year one. The product mix is not published, so the estimate uses 3.1. Some extra implant users would otherwise have used another modern method. A 2020 evaluation found implants were the main driver of rising modern contraceptive use in 11 of 12 sub-Saharan African countries with repeated surveys, so the estimate assumes, as a judgment, that 60 percent of extra implant use was contraception that would not otherwise have happened. GiveWell values a year of modern contraception that would not otherwise have happened at about 0.7 units of value, which gives about 1.3 units per extra implant.

Extra implants also had to be inserted, at a cost to health systems and other funders. A costing of a postpartum implant scale-up in Kigali, Rwanda, found $77 per insertion including the device, training and demand creation, and routine services cost less, so the estimate assumes $30 per insertion excluding the device, a judgment, valued at what that money would otherwise have done. That leaves about 1.1 units per extra implant.

**A released dollar.** Money buyers no longer needed for implants went to their next priority, in family planning or elsewhere. The estimate values it at 0.007 units per dollar, about twice the benchmark. GiveWell's staff use this figure for Gavi spending that a grant displaces, and 0.015 for the Global Fund. Choosing it is a judgment.

**Probability and contribution.** The deal happened, so in retrospect the probability of success is one. An estimate made in 2012 would have weighted these outcomes by the chance that suppliers delivered and demand materialized. The estimate covers the partners together and assumes, as a judgment, that no comparable deal would have happened without them, for instance through the largest buyers negotiating on their own.

The public record does not give the partners' combined cost. The guarantees' face value ran to hundreds of millions of dollars, and Sida, Sweden's development agency and one of the guarantors, put the total at $409 million. But both suppliers delivered more than the agreed volumes in 2013, and no public source reports a payout. The costs that were certainly incurred are the <Term>catalyst</Term>'s work, the coordination of procurement, the in-country training the partners funded, and whatever it cost the guarantors to hold the commitments. Unless those exceeded about $60 million, the deal met the bar in every cell of the table, on the judgments above. At a combined cost of $25 million, chosen for illustration, the central estimate is about 40x the benchmark, with a range of about 15x to 220x.

Within each column of the table, the break-even cost varies by a factor of three to six across the cases for buyer spending, and only the slow price path changes it as much. Because an extra implant is worth far more than a released dollar, most of the uncertainty comes from how buyers split the gain between more implants and money for other uses.

### What the Estimate Leaves Out

- GiveWell's value of a year of contraception has a 25th to 75th percentile range of about 0.3 to 1.1 units. Across that range, an extra implant is worth about 0.35 to 1.8 units, against the 1.1 used here.
- It gives no value to anything after the counterfactual date, including what manufacturers learned, or to savings when users switched from costlier methods.
- It applies no discounting over 2013 to 2020; discounting would lower the slow cases most.
- It counts no guarantee payout, since none is reported. The partners' costs enter only through the break-even figures.
- Shipments measure supply rather than use, though the 2020 evaluation found no evidence of overstocking.
