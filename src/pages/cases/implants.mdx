## The Trap

Before 2013, Bayer's Jadelle sold for about $18 per unit and Merck's Implanon for between $16.50 and $18, depending on volume thresholds. The Clinton Health Access Initiative described the market as a high-price, low-volume trap. Suppliers kept unit prices high because orders were small and unpredictable, and orders stayed small because prices were high. That made implants hard to budget for, for donors and ministries alike.

## The Intervention

In 2013, a partnership including the Gates Foundation guaranteed a minimum volume of purchases. Bayer cut Jadelle's price from $18 to $8.50 per unit, effective January 1, 2013, in more than 50 countries, under an agreement intended to reach more than 27 million women over six years. Merck reduced Implanon to the same price.

## Lever Accounting

The price drop is usually attributed to "the volume guarantee." Decomposing it by <Term>lever</Term> shows that several mechanisms were at work, with different durability.

- **Scale and learning.** Multiyear volume commitments let both manufacturers invest in capacity and run at higher utilization. The economies of scale hold as long as volume persists at the new price; what the manufacturers learned from producing more largely persists regardless.
- **Risk transfer.** The guarantee removed the demand uncertainty that had been priced into earlier offers. This holds if demand proved real, so that the guarantee was not drawn on.
- **Process improvement.** Harmonizing packaging specifications that had been fragmented across buyers increased throughput on packaging lines. This holds almost regardless of what happens next.
- **Competition, delayed.** Shanghai Dahua's implant, distributed as Levoplant, was prequalified by the World Health Organization in June 2017 and offered at $6.90 per unit in 2018. Competition holds only as long as entrants remain viable at the lower price.

## Reading It Through the Framework

**Diagnosis.** Donors and governments were already buying implants for women who could not pay full price, so demand existed. What held the market back was the trap: a transitional gap. The guarantee navigated it, and left the buyers to carry on buying at the lower price.

**Durability.** The levers that fired hold under different conditions. Process improvement holds almost regardless of what happens next. Scale holds while volume holds, and the learning it brought largely persists. Competition holds only while the third supplier stays viable at the lower price. Whether risk transfer held depends on whether the guarantee was drawn on, which the public record reviewed here does not settle.

**Value per unit.** Because donors and governments were buying, most of the value of the price cut flowed through their budgets. Each dollar saved could buy more implants or fund other family planning, so the value depends on what payers did with the savings.

**The counterfactual.** A lower-cost implant was already moving toward prequalification, so some price decline was likely coming. The honest claim is the acceleration the <Term>volume guarantee</Term> caused, years of lower prices and higher volume earlier than otherwise, plus any permanent difference.

**Exposure.** The low price rests partly on volume financed by donors and governments. If that volume falls, economies of scale are the part most likely to unwind, and competition may weaken if lower volume squeezes a supplier out. The process improvements and the learning should largely hold.
