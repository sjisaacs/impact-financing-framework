## What Happened

British Columbia introduced its consumer carbon tax in 2008, and over time paired it with a climate action tax credit to offset its cost to households. In March 2025, after the federal government removed its consumer carbon pricing requirement, the province announced it would eliminate its own consumer tax. Legislation set the rate to zero effective April 1, 2025, and the household credit ended with a final payment that month.

The provincial government said people should not have to choose between climate action and affordability, and that the tax had become divisive. It estimated the average family would otherwise have paid about $410 in carbon tax in 2025 to 2026. The province's filings projected net revenue about $2 billion lower in that fiscal year, after accounting for the end of the credit.

## Analysis

**Diagnosis.** A <Term t="Carbon Tax">carbon price</Term> is the textbook response to a structural gap with a destination <Term>externality</Term>. It reshapes the terrain by making the harmful activity costlier, and it must stay in place for as long as the externality exists.

**Durability.** The design included features often recommended to make carbon prices durable, including returning revenue to households. The tax still ended after 17 years, and the case does not show whether those features extended its life.

**Cost-effectiveness.** How long a structural payer will keep paying is a central question for anyone financing work that depends on it, since investments such as capacity built to serve a price signal can be stranded when the payer stops. An estimate should weight scenarios in which the payer persists, weakens or ends.

**What the framework does not do.** It takes no position on whether the decision was right. Carbon pricing involves trade-offs among climate goals, affordability and political consent that reasonable people weigh differently. The framework notes only that structural solutions carry political risk and that the ratio should include it.
