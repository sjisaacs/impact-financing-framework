## The Design

All vaccines bought under the commitment were initially purchased at $7 per dose. That price combined each manufacturer's tail price, capped at $3.50 and paid by Gavi, with a top-up from the commitment's funds. The top-up applied to roughly the first 20 percent of each supplier's contracted doses. In return, manufacturers committed to supply a share of forecast demand for ten years, continuing at the tail price once their share of the funds was used.

## What It Achieved, and the Critique

An independent evaluation for Gavi found that the commitment contributed to increased supply and uptake of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines in developing countries. Gavi noted that the tail price ceiling had been set to allow more than one manufacturer to participate, and later negotiated reductions.

Critics drew a different conclusion from the same history. Médecins Sans Frontières argued that a vaccine already existed, so the commitment did little to accelerate research, and that much of the money functioned as a subsidy to the only two suppliers, Pfizer and GlaxoSmithKline, until a third entered in late 2019. It also argued the tail price was higher than countries could sustain as Gavi support phased out.

## Analysis

**Diagnosis.** The buyers existed: Gavi and countries would buy the vaccine. The gap was transitional, over capacity and price in these markets. <Term t="Advance Market Commitment">Advance market commitments</Term> were conceived for products that do not yet exist, and this one was applied to a vaccine that already existed.

**Levers.** The commitment worked mainly through risk transfer on market existence and capacity, plus scale. Its most durable feature was the long-term tail price ceiling, which persists after the top-up funds are spent.

**The counterfactual.** Most of the disagreement is about the <Term>counterfactual</Term>, which determines how much of the later supply the commitment can claim. If manufacturers would have supplied these markets at similar prices only a few years later, the commitment's impact is the value of those years of earlier coverage, minus the premium paid. If they would have supplied much later, at higher prices, or in smaller volumes, the impact is far larger. Supporters and critics agree on most of the facts and differ over the dashed line in the [impact figure](/cost-effectiveness/#why-durability-multiplies-impact).

**Lesson for design.** Pull mechanisms are easiest to evaluate, and most likely to be cost-effective, where the counterfactual is clear, as with products that do not yet exist or markets that suppliers have shown they will not enter. Where a product already exists, a volume guarantee or ceiling price agreement may bring supply forward as quickly at lower expected cost.
