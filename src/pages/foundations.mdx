### The Core Problem

Markets are very good at directing resources toward profitable activities, and **what is profitable is often not what is best for society.**

Polluting is often cheaper than not polluting. Selling medicine to rich countries pays better than selling to poor ones, and extracting resources is more profitable than preserving them. Markets follow financial returns, which match social benefit only some of the time.

### Existing Approaches

The usual responses each have strengths and limits.

<div class="not-prose grid md:grid-cols-2 gap-4 my-6">
  <div class="bg-white p-5 rounded-xl border border-slate-200"><div class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Charity and aid</div><p class="text-sm text-slate-600">Essential, but can't scale to trillions. Depends on ongoing donor commitment, and rarely changes the underlying economics.</p></div>
  <div class="bg-white p-5 rounded-xl border border-slate-200"><div class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Regulation and mandates</div><p class="text-sm text-slate-600">Powerful, and often the right tool, but slow to change and hard to enforce across borders. It lasts only as long as the political support behind it.</p></div>
  <div class="bg-white p-5 rounded-xl border border-slate-200"><div class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Waiting for markets to adapt</div><p class="text-sm text-slate-600">Sometimes they do, but often too slowly, and not at all when the economics are structurally wrong.</p></div>
  <div class="bg-white p-5 rounded-xl border border-slate-200"><div class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Government provision</div><p class="text-sm text-slate-600">Works well for some goods, but has its own inefficiencies and can crowd out private innovation.</p></div>
</div>

<div class="not-prose bg-blue-50 border border-blue-100 rounded-xl p-6 md:p-8 my-8">
  <h4 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-3">Reshaping Markets</h4>
  <p class="text-slate-700 mb-3">Public and philanthropic capital can also be used to <strong>reshape the markets themselves</strong>, an option that remains underused. It can change the economic landscape so that private capital, far larger than aid budgets, flows toward beneficial outcomes on its own.</p>
  <p class="text-slate-700 mb-3">Markets can't solve everything, and some problems require direct public provision or regulation (the framework treats regulation as one of the ways to reshape a landscape). When market interventions work, they can achieve scale and sustainability that philanthropy alone cannot.</p>
  <p class="text-slate-900 font-semibold">This framework provides a systematic way to decide when these tools work, and how much they are worth per dollar.</p>
</div>

<Section id="key-concepts" tone="slate" title="Key Concepts" intro="The framework relies on three concepts, defined here.">
<div class="not-prose space-y-6">
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-2xl text-slate-900 mb-3 flex items-center gap-3"><span class="w-7 h-7 bg-blue-100 rounded-full flex items-center justify-center text-blue-700 text-sm font-bold font-sans">1</span>Capital</h3>
    <p class="text-slate-600 mb-3">Financial resources deployed to achieve objectives. Capital exists on a spectrum. At one end, commercial capital (pension funds, banks, private equity) seeks market-rate returns. At the other, philanthropic capital (grants, donations) accepts no financial return in exchange for social impact. Concessional capital covers everything short of commercial capital, from below-market loans to grants. It accepts less than it could earn elsewhere, often in order to attract other investment.</p>
    <p class="text-slate-600">Capital allocators decide based on risk (likelihood of loss), return (expected financial gain) and, increasingly, impact (expected social or environmental outcomes). To design an effective intervention, you need to know where each type of capital is on this spectrum and what drives its decisions.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-2xl text-slate-900 mb-3 flex items-center gap-3"><span class="w-7 h-7 bg-blue-100 rounded-full flex items-center justify-center text-blue-700 text-sm font-bold font-sans">2</span>Markets and Their Failures</h3>
    <p class="text-slate-600 mb-3">Markets are systems where buyers and sellers find each other and agree on prices. They allocate resources well. When something is scarce and valuable, its price rises and draws in more supply. But markets fail in predictable ways.</p>
    <ul class="text-slate-600 space-y-2 mb-3 list-disc pl-5">
      <li><strong class="text-slate-800">Externalities</strong> occur when costs or benefits fall on people outside the transaction. Examples include pollution, which harms neighbors who didn't buy the product, and vaccination, which also protects people who weren't vaccinated.</li>
      <li><strong class="text-slate-800">Information failures</strong> occur when buyers or sellers lack important knowledge. An investor may not understand a new technology's potential, or a consumer may not know a product's true quality.</li>
      <li><strong class="text-slate-800">Coordination failures</strong> occur when actors would benefit from collective action but can't achieve it independently. For example, suppliers won't invest without assured demand, and buyers won't commit without assured supply.</li>
    </ul>
    <p class="text-slate-600 mb-3">Which intervention will work depends on which failure is present.</p>
    <p class="text-slate-600">Needs without purchasing power are a different matter. They go unmet even when markets work efficiently, because the people who have them cannot pay. Governments and donors usually address this distribution problem by buying on others' behalf.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-2xl text-slate-900 mb-3 flex items-center gap-3"><span class="w-7 h-7 bg-blue-100 rounded-full flex items-center justify-center text-blue-700 text-sm font-bold font-sans">3</span>Market Intervention</h3>
    <p class="text-slate-600 mb-3">An action that <em>changes</em> the incentives, risks or constraints facing market actors, so that they behave differently than they otherwise would.</p>
    <div class="grid md:grid-cols-2 gap-4 text-sm">
      <div class="bg-slate-50 p-4 rounded-lg"><div class="font-medium text-slate-800 mb-1">Examples of interventions</div><ul class="text-slate-600 space-y-1"><li>Guaranteeing purchases if a supplier invests</li><li>Putting a price on carbon emissions</li><li>Subsidizing a product to make it affordable</li><li>Absorbing risk that private capital won't bear</li><li>Paying for verified outcomes</li></ul></div>
      <div class="bg-slate-50 p-4 rounded-lg"><div class="font-medium text-slate-800 mb-1">Interventions vary by duration</div><ul class="text-slate-600 space-y-1"><li><strong>Temporary:</strong> a guarantee that proves a market works, then exits</li><li><strong>Ongoing:</strong> a carbon price that permanently corrects an externality</li></ul><p class="text-slate-600 mt-2">Which one you need is the framework's first question.</p></div>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="callout my-8"><span class="label">A note on concessionality</span>The difference between what capital accepts and what it could earn elsewhere is an implicit subsidy. Concessional capital is scarce, so a dollar of subsidy spent where it wasn't needed cannot be spent on problems that require it. The framework therefore measures everything as <a href="/cost-effectiveness/">durable impact per concessional dollar</a>.</div>
</Section>

<Section id="common-mistakes" title="Common Mistakes" intro="Market interventions often fail in predictable ways.">
<div class="not-prose space-y-4 mb-10">
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
  <div class="flex items-start gap-4">
  <div class="w-10 h-10 bg-red-100 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center flex-shrink-0"><span class="text-red-600 font-bold">1</span></div>
  <div>
    <h3 class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Confusing Temporary and Permanent Problems</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-2">Some markets need only a push to get started, after which they sustain themselves. Others have structural problems that require permanent intervention. Treating a temporary problem as permanent wastes resources, and the opposite mistake leads to failure when support is withdrawn.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-emerald-700 bg-emerald-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>Framework element: </strong><a class="underline" href="/framework/#the-walk-away-test">the walk-away test</a> tells you whether the outcome would last if support ended.</p>
  </div>
  </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
  <div class="flex items-start gap-4">
  <div class="w-10 h-10 bg-red-100 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center flex-shrink-0"><span class="text-red-600 font-bold">2</span></div>
  <div>
    <h3 class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Tool-First Thinking</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-2">"We want to launch an impact bond" is backwards. Starting with the instrument instead of the problem produces solutions that don't fit the market failure.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-emerald-700 bg-emerald-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>Framework element: </strong><a class="underline" href="/framework/mechanisms/">mechanisms</a>. Design for a lever whose effect will last after exit, then choose an instrument that works through it.</p>
  </div>
  </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
  <div class="flex items-start gap-4">
  <div class="w-10 h-10 bg-red-100 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center flex-shrink-0"><span class="text-red-600 font-bold">3</span></div>
  <div>
    <h3 class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Ignoring Market Structure</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-2">Focusing on financial structures without understanding who makes decisions in the market and what influences them. A subsidy targeted at manufacturers may do nothing if the bottleneck is at the distributor level.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-emerald-700 bg-emerald-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>Framework element: </strong><a class="underline" href="/framework/mechanisms/#the-catalyst">the catalyst</a>, the organization that understands where value is created and decisions are made.</p>
  </div>
  </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
  <div class="flex items-start gap-4">
  <div class="w-10 h-10 bg-red-100 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center flex-shrink-0"><span class="text-red-600 font-bold">4</span></div>
  <div>
    <h3 class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Using the Wrong Amount of Subsidy</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-2">Providing grants when loans would work, or loans when the market needed only a guarantee. Funders also make the reverse mistake and spend too little for any lever to have a lasting effect.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-emerald-700 bg-emerald-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>Framework element: </strong><a class="underline" href="/cost-effectiveness/#precision-and-durability">precision</a>, measured in expected cost per unit of durable impact instead of dollars committed.</p>
  </div>
  </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
  <div class="flex items-start gap-4">
  <div class="w-10 h-10 bg-red-100 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center flex-shrink-0"><span class="text-red-600 font-bold">5</span></div>
  <div>
    <h3 class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Claiming the Whole Price Drop</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-2">Funders often claim credit for every unit sold at a lower price, years after the fact. Much of that volume would have been sold anyway, and most deals have several funders who each claim the same result.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-emerald-700 bg-emerald-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>Framework element: </strong><a class="underline" href="/cost-effectiveness/#duration-beyond-the-counterfactual">the counterfactual</a> and <a class="underline" href="/cost-effectiveness/#counterfactual-contribution">counterfactual contribution</a>.</p>
  </div>
  </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
  <div class="flex items-start gap-4">
  <div class="w-10 h-10 bg-red-100 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center flex-shrink-0"><span class="text-red-600 font-bold">6</span></div>
  <div>
    <h3 class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Reinventing the Wheel</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-2">Practitioners in health, climate and development finance have developed similar solutions independently, with different terminology, conferences and journals. Lessons and proven approaches are not carried across sectors, and mistakes get repeated.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-emerald-700 bg-emerald-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>Framework element: </strong>a shared structure. The same two questions apply in every sector.</p>
  </div>
  </div>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="not-prose bg-gradient-to-br from-blue-900 to-blue-800 text-white rounded-xl p-6 md:p-8">
  <h3 class="font-serif text-2xl mb-3">Market Shaping, Blended Finance and Carbon Finance</h3>
  <p class="text-blue-100 mb-3">Practitioners treat market shaping (health), blended finance (development) and carbon finance (climate) as separate disciplines. Each has its own expertise, but all three have to decide whether a gap is transitional or structural, and what will remain when the funder steps back.</p>
  <p class="text-blue-100 mb-5">Global health has mostly done transitional work in markets with established buyers, while climate has mostly had to create the payer.</p>
