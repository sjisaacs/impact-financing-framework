### The Core Problem

Markets are extraordinarily good at directing resources toward profitable activities. That's their superpower. But **"profitable" and "good for society" aren't the same thing.**

Polluting is often cheaper than not polluting. Selling medicine to rich countries pays better than selling to poor ones. Extracting resources is more profitable than preserving them. Markets optimize for financial returns, which sometimes align with social benefit and sometimes don't.

### So What Do We Do?

The traditional answers each have their place, and each has limits.

<div class="not-prose grid md:grid-cols-2 gap-4 my-6">
  <div class="bg-white p-5 rounded-xl border border-slate-200"><div class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Charity and aid</div><p class="text-sm text-slate-600">Essential, but can't scale to trillions. Depends on ongoing donor commitment, and rarely changes the underlying economics.</p></div>
  <div class="bg-white p-5 rounded-xl border border-slate-200"><div class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Regulation and mandates</div><p class="text-sm text-slate-600">Powerful, and often the right tool. But slow to change, hard to enforce across borders, and only as durable as the politics behind it.</p></div>
  <div class="bg-white p-5 rounded-xl border border-slate-200"><div class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Waiting for markets to adapt</div><p class="text-sm text-slate-600">Sometimes they do, but often too slowly, and not at all when the economics are structurally wrong.</p></div>
  <div class="bg-white p-5 rounded-xl border border-slate-200"><div class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Government provision</div><p class="text-sm text-slate-600">Works well for some goods, but has its own inefficiencies and can crowd out private innovation.</p></div>
</div>

<div class="not-prose bg-blue-50 border border-blue-100 rounded-xl p-6 md:p-8 my-8">
  <h4 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-3">A Different Approach</h4>
  <p class="text-slate-700 mb-3">There's another lever that remains underused: <strong>reshaping the markets themselves</strong>. Public and philanthropic capital can do more than fund good outcomes directly. It can change the economic landscape so that private capital, vastly larger than aid budgets, flows toward beneficial outcomes on its own.</p>
  <p class="text-slate-700 mb-3">This isn't a silver bullet. Markets can't solve everything, and some problems require direct public provision or regulation; the framework treats regulation as one of the ways to reshape a landscape. But when market interventions work, they can achieve scale and sustainability that pure philanthropy cannot.</p>
  <p class="text-slate-900 font-semibold">This framework provides a systematic way to decide when these tools work, and how much they are worth per dollar.</p>
</div>
</Section>

<Section id="key-concepts" tone="slate" title="Key Concepts" intro="Before diving into the framework, we need a shared vocabulary. These three concepts are the essential building blocks.">
<div class="not-prose space-y-6">
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-2xl text-slate-900 mb-3 flex items-center gap-3"><span class="w-7 h-7 bg-blue-100 rounded-full flex items-center justify-center text-blue-700 text-sm font-bold font-sans">1</span>Capital</h3>
    <p class="text-slate-600 mb-3">Financial resources deployed to achieve objectives. Capital exists on a spectrum. At one end, commercial capital (pension funds, banks, private equity) seeks market-rate returns. At the other, philanthropic capital (grants, donations) accepts no financial return in exchange for social impact. In between lies concessional capital: money willing to accept below-market returns to catalyze broader investment.</p>
    <p class="text-slate-600">Capital allocators decide based on risk (likelihood of loss), return (expected financial gain) and, increasingly, impact (expected social or environmental outcomes). Understanding where different types of capital sit on this spectrum, and what drives their decisions, is essential to designing effective interventions.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-2xl text-slate-900 mb-3 flex items-center gap-3"><span class="w-7 h-7 bg-blue-100 rounded-full flex items-center justify-center text-blue-700 text-sm font-bold font-sans">2</span>Markets and Their Failures</h3>
    <p class="text-slate-600 mb-3">Markets are systems where buyers and sellers find each other and agree on prices. They're remarkably efficient at allocating resources: when something is scarce and valuable, prices rise, attracting more supply. But markets fail in predictable ways.</p>
    <ul class="text-slate-600 space-y-2 mb-3 list-disc pl-5">
      <li><strong class="text-slate-800">Externalities</strong> occur when costs or benefits fall on people outside the transaction. Pollution harms neighbors who didn't buy the product; vaccines protect people who weren't vaccinated.</li>
      <li><strong class="text-slate-800">Information failures</strong> occur when buyers or sellers lack crucial knowledge. Investors may not understand a new technology's potential; consumers may not know a product's true quality.</li>
      <li><strong class="text-slate-800">Coordination failures</strong> occur when actors would benefit from collective action but can't achieve it independently. Suppliers won't invest without assured demand; buyers won't commit without assured supply.</li>
      <li><strong class="text-slate-800">Needs without purchasing power</strong> go unmet even when markets work efficiently, simply because the people who have them cannot pay.</li>
    </ul>
    <p class="text-slate-600">Understanding which failure is at play determines which intervention will work.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-2xl text-slate-900 mb-3 flex items-center gap-3"><span class="w-7 h-7 bg-blue-100 rounded-full flex items-center justify-center text-blue-700 text-sm font-bold font-sans">3</span>Market Intervention</h3>
    <p class="text-slate-600 mb-3">An action that changes the economic calculus facing market actors, so they behave differently than they otherwise would. The key word is <em>changes</em>: an intervention alters incentives, risks or constraints in a way that redirects behavior.</p>
    <div class="grid md:grid-cols-2 gap-4 text-sm">
      <div class="bg-slate-50 p-4 rounded-lg"><div class="font-medium text-slate-800 mb-1">Examples of interventions</div><ul class="text-slate-600 space-y-1"><li>Guaranteeing purchases if a supplier invests</li><li>Putting a price on carbon emissions</li><li>Subsidizing a product to make it affordable</li><li>Absorbing risk that private capital won't bear</li><li>Paying for verified outcomes</li></ul></div>
      <div class="bg-slate-50 p-4 rounded-lg"><div class="font-medium text-slate-800 mb-1">Interventions vary by duration</div><ul class="text-slate-600 space-y-1"><li><strong>Temporary:</strong> a guarantee that proves a market works, then exits</li><li><strong>Ongoing:</strong> a carbon price that permanently corrects an externality</li></ul><p class="text-slate-600 mt-2">Which one you need is the framework's first question.</p></div>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="callout my-8"><span class="label">A note on concessionality</span>The gap between what capital accepts and what it could earn elsewhere is effectively a subsidy. Concessional capital is scarce, and every dollar of subsidy deployed where it wasn't needed is a dollar unavailable for problems that truly require it. This is why the framework measures everything as <a href="/cost-effectiveness/">durable impact per concessional dollar</a>.</div>
</Section>

<Section id="common-mistakes" title="Common Mistakes" intro="Market interventions fail frequently, often in predictable ways. Understanding these patterns shows why systematic thinking matters.">
<div class="not-prose space-y-4 mb-10">
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
  <div class="flex items-start gap-4">
  <div class="w-10 h-10 bg-red-100 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center flex-shrink-0"><span class="text-red-600 font-bold">1</span></div>
  <div>
    <h3 class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Confusing Temporary and Permanent Problems</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-2">Some markets just need a push to get started, then they sustain themselves. Others have structural problems that require permanent intervention. Treating a temporary problem as permanent wastes resources. Treating a permanent problem as temporary sets up failure when support is withdrawn.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-emerald-700 bg-emerald-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>Framework element: </strong><a class="underline" href="/framework/#the-walk-away-test">the walk-away test</a> tells you whether the outcome would hold if support ended.</p>
  </div>
  </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
  <div class="flex items-start gap-4">
  <div class="w-10 h-10 bg-red-100 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center flex-shrink-0"><span class="text-red-600 font-bold">2</span></div>
  <div>
    <h3 class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Tool-First Thinking</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-2">"We want to launch an impact bond" is backwards. Starting with the instrument rather than the problem produces solutions that don't fit the actual market failure.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-emerald-700 bg-emerald-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>Framework element: </strong><a class="underline" href="/framework/mechanisms/">mechanisms</a>. Design for the lever that will hold after exit, then choose the instrument that fires it.</p>
  </div>
  </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
  <div class="flex items-start gap-4">
  <div class="w-10 h-10 bg-red-100 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center flex-shrink-0"><span class="text-red-600 font-bold">3</span></div>
  <div>
    <h3 class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Ignoring Market Structure</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-2">Focusing on financial structures without understanding who makes decisions in the market and what influences them. A subsidy targeted at manufacturers may do nothing if the bottleneck is at the distributor level.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-emerald-700 bg-emerald-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>Framework element: </strong><a class="underline" href="/framework/mechanisms/#the-catalyst">the catalyst</a>, the organization that understands where value is created and decisions are made.</p>
  </div>
  </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
  <div class="flex items-start gap-4">
  <div class="w-10 h-10 bg-red-100 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center flex-shrink-0"><span class="text-red-600 font-bold">4</span></div>
  <div>
    <h3 class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Using the Wrong Amount of Subsidy</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-2">Providing grants when loans would work, or loans when the market just needed a guarantee. But also the reverse: spending too little to fire a lever that lasts. Over-subsidizing wastes scarce capital; under-subsidizing means the intervention fails or fades.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-emerald-700 bg-emerald-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>Framework element: </strong><a class="underline" href="/cost-effectiveness/#precision-and-durability">precision</a>, measured in expected cost per unit of durable impact, not in dollars committed.</p>
  </div>
  </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
  <div class="flex items-start gap-4">
  <div class="w-10 h-10 bg-red-100 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center flex-shrink-0"><span class="text-red-600 font-bold">5</span></div>
  <div>
    <h3 class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Claiming the Whole Price Drop</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-2">Interventions often take credit for every unit sold at a lower price, years after the fact. Much of that volume would have arrived anyway, and most deals have several funders who each claim the same result.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-emerald-700 bg-emerald-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>Framework element: </strong><a class="underline" href="/cost-effectiveness/#duration-beyond-the-counterfactual">the counterfactual</a> and <a class="underline" href="/cost-effectiveness/#share-attributable">attribution</a>.</p>
  </div>
  </div>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
  <div class="flex items-start gap-4">
  <div class="w-10 h-10 bg-red-100 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center flex-shrink-0"><span class="text-red-600 font-bold">6</span></div>
  <div>
    <h3 class="font-semibold text-slate-900 mb-2">Reinventing the Wheel</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-2">Practitioners in health, climate and development finance have developed similar solutions independently, using different terminology, attending different conferences and reading different journals. Lessons aren't shared, mistakes are repeated, and proven approaches aren't adapted across sectors.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-emerald-700 bg-emerald-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>Framework element: </strong>a shared structure. The same two questions apply in every sector.</p>
  </div>
  </div>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="not-prose bg-gradient-to-br from-blue-900 to-blue-800 text-white rounded-xl p-6 md:p-8">
  <h3 class="font-serif text-2xl mb-3">One Structure, Many Names</h3>
  <p class="text-blue-100 mb-3">Practitioners use market shaping (health), blended finance (development) and carbon finance (climate) as if they were separate disciplines. They are distinct fields with their own expertise, but they answer the same two questions: is the gap transitional or structural, and what will remain when the funder steps back?</p>
  <p class="text-blue-100 mb-5">Global health has mostly done transitional work inside markets with established payers. Climate has mostly had to build the payer. Same structure, different stages.</p>
