<Section id="two-kinds-of-gap" title="Two Kinds of Gap, Two Kinds of Solution" intro="There are two fundamental kinds of gap between what a market does and what we want it to do. Which one applies determines what to use, how long to intervene and what success looks like." wide>

### The Walk-Away Test

If a fully successful intervention is withdrawn and the market holds its new position, the gap was transitional, and if the market slides back, the gap was structural. The test asks whether the outcome we want is self-enforcing, which shows whether the intervention can end and so whether impact can continue after spending stops.

Answering it takes evidence on how unit cost at scale compares with what the buyers who will remain can sustainably pay, whether new suppliers could enter at that price and whether high volume is even desirable. For novel antibiotics, stewardship aims to keep use low, so a gap that looks transitional is structural.

<Section id="learning-curves" tone="slate" title="When Costs Fall with Volume">

Where unit costs fall with cumulative production, a market has two stable positions: low volume at high cost, and high volume at low cost. The result is a transitional gap with an unusually high ridge, which wears down as capital flows through it because each unit made lowers the cost of the next. Solar power in the late 2000s and electric vehicles in the mid-2010s are familiar examples, each too expensive at first, with every unit deployed driving costs down.

<Fig takeaway="Learning curves are transitional gaps whose ridge erodes as volume grows." caption="Lighter lines are earlier. Support is needed only until cumulative volume brings costs below what buyers will pay.">
  <LearningCurve />
</Fig>

Solar also shows how the two kinds of gap interact. Its unpriced climate benefit was a structural gap, yet once costs fell far enough, new solar often won on cost alone, and the structural gap stopped being a critical barrier for many projects. Before committing to a permanent subsidy, funders should ask whether lower costs could make it unnecessary.

</Section>

<Section id="the-externality" title="What the Externality Is" intro="Economics has a standard name for the difference between the two landscapes. Its geometry shows how big an intervention must be and whether it is worth doing.">

The externality, broadly defined, is the difference between social and private value at every point. The two low points are in different places only if that difference has a slope at the market's low point. There, the financial landscape is flat by definition, so any tilt left in the social landscape is exactly the marginal externality. Economists call this gap between private and social value at the margin a **wedge**. The idea goes back to Arthur Pigou, the Cambridge economist who argued in *The Economics of Welfare* (1920) that a tax or subsidy equal to the divergence would bring private decisions into line with social ones. Corrections of this kind are still called Pigouvian taxes.

It follows that an externality, however large, causes misalignment only if it changes as the activity changes. If a factory imposed the same fixed harm whatever it produced, the social landscape would be the financial one shifted, the low points would coincide, and no corrective price would change behavior.

### Path Externalities and Destination Externalities

Transitional gaps can have externalities too. The two kinds of gap differ in where the externality matters: on the way to the better outcome, or at the outcome itself.

Two classic ideas in economics describe externalities on the way. In 1943 the development economist Paul Rosenstein-Rodan argued that poor regions stay poor partly because no single factory is worth building alone, since each would succeed only if others were built at the same time to supply it and buy from it. He later named the remedy a "big push" of coordinated investment. The problem he described is a <Term>coordination failure</Term>, in which one firm's investment benefits others who do not pay for it. In 1962 Kenneth Arrow, who went on to win the Nobel Prize in economics, showed that productivity rises with cumulative production, which he called <Term>learning by doing</Term>, and that the knowledge gained spills over to other firms. These spillovers help build the ridge in a transitional gap, and they stop being a barrier once capital reaches the better valley.

A structural gap has its externality at the destination. The social landscape is still tilted at the market's low point, and stays tilted while the underlying economics persist. The walk-away test is a practical way to tell the two apart.

### Two Sources of the Wedge

1. **Effects on third parties.** Emissions, infection and antimicrobial resistance are classic externalities, corrected by pricing the harm or paying for the benefit.
2. **Value no one can be excluded from.** Markets underprovide public goods, from disease surveillance to research, because no one can be charged.

Tobacco is often cited as a corrective-tax case, but it is largely an <Term>internality</Term>, meaning harm that smokers do to their own future selves through addiction and present bias.

<div class="callout"><span class="label">Not a market failure: needs without purchasing power</span>When a poor patient cannot pay for treatment, the market may be working efficiently given incomes. What it delivers differs from what society wants because willingness to pay depends on ability to pay. The shortfall is a question of distribution, and treating it as structural would make almost every market structural. Donors, governments and pooled funds address it by buying on others' behalf. The framework treats those buyers as part of the market, and asks what keeps that market from its best outcome (<a href="/framework/payers/#buyers-for-others">Payers</a>).</div>
