<Section id="two-kinds-of-gap" title="Two Kinds of Gap, Two Kinds of Solution" intro="There are two fundamental kinds of gap between what a market does and what we want it to do. Knowing which you face determines what to use, how long to intervene, and what success looks like." wide>

### The Walk-Away Test

Imagine the intervention has fully succeeded and is then withdrawn. If the market holds its new position, the gap was transitional. If it slides back, the gap was structural. The test asks one question: is the outcome we want self-enforcing? That alone decides whether an intervention can end, and so whether its impact can outlast its cost.

Answering it honestly is empirical work: compare unit cost at scale with what the buyers who will remain can sustainably pay, ask whether new suppliers could enter at that price, and ask whether high volume is even desirable. Novel antibiotics show why the last question matters. Stewardship means high volume is never supposed to arrive, so a gap that looks transitional is structural.

<Section id="gaps-not-markets" tone="slate" title="Diagnose Gaps, Not Markets">

Most markets contain both kinds of gap, so the walk-away test applies to each gap rather than to the market as a whole. Donor financing for contraceptives in low-income countries is a permanent payer, a structural solution that has been in place for decades. The 2013 implant volume guarantee solved a transitional problem inside it, moving that subsidized market from high price and low volume to low price and high volume ([see the case](/cases/implants/)).

The order of diagnosis matters. First ask who must pay permanently, and how much, for the outcome to exist at all. Then ask what keeps the market from reaching the best outcome that payer can sustain. [Payers](/framework/payers/) covers the first question and [Mechanisms](/framework/mechanisms/) the second.

</Section>

<Section id="learning-curves" title="When Costs Fall with Volume">

Where unit costs fall with cumulative production, a market has two stable positions: low volume at high cost, and high volume at low cost. That is a transitional gap with an unusually high ridge, and the ridge wears down as capital flows through it, because each unit made lowers the cost of the next. Solar power in the late 2000s and electric vehicles in the mid-2010s are the familiar examples: too expensive at first, but every unit deployed drove costs down.

Solar also shows how the two kinds of gap interact. Its unpriced climate benefit was a structural gap, yet once costs fell far enough, new solar often won on cost alone and that gap stopped binding for many projects. Solving a transitional gap can make a structural one matter less, which is worth knowing before committing to a permanent subsidy.

</Section>

<Section id="the-externality" tone="slate" title="What the Externality Is" intro="Economics has a precise name for the difference between the two landscapes. Getting its geometry right tells you how big an intervention must be and whether it is worth doing.">

The externality, broadly defined, is the difference between social and private value at every point. What separates the two low points, though, is not the size of that difference but its slope at the market's low point. There, the financial landscape is flat by definition. Any tilt left in the social landscape at that point is exactly the marginal externality, which the framework calls the **wedge**.

One consequence is easy to miss. A large externality that does not vary with the activity moves nothing. If a factory imposed the same fixed harm whatever it produced, the social landscape would be the financial one shifted, the low points would coincide, and no corrective price would change behavior. Misalignment requires a gap that changes as the activity changes.

### Path Externalities and Destination Externalities

Externalities are not unique to structural gaps, which is why the distinction turns on where they bind rather than whether they exist. A <Term>coordination failure</Term> exists because one firm's investment benefits others who do not pay for it; Rosenstein-Rodan's argument for a "big push" rests on exactly these spillovers. Learning by doing leaks to competitors, as Arrow showed. These externalities live on the path: they build the ridge, and they stop binding once capital reaches the better valley.

A structural gap has its externality at the destination. The social landscape is still tilted at the market's low point, and will stay tilted for as long as the underlying economics hold. The walk-away test is a practical check on exactly this distinction.

### Three Sources of the Wedge

1. **Effects on third parties.** Emissions, infection, antimicrobial resistance. The classic externality, corrected by a price on the harm or a payment for the benefit.
2. **Value no one can be excluded from.** Public goods, from disease surveillance to research. Markets underprovide them because no one can be charged.
3. **Needs not backed by purchasing power.** When a poor patient cannot pay for treatment, the market may be efficient given incomes; social value differs because willingness to pay is weighted by income. The remedy is a transfer that someone must fund indefinitely, not a price correction, so it is worth naming separately.

Much of the structural gap in global health is the third kind. Tobacco, often cited as a corrective-tax case, is largely something else: an <Term>internality</Term>, harm smokers do to their own future selves through addiction and present bias.
