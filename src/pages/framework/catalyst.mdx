<Section id="where-the-model-ends" title="Where the Model Ends">

The rest of this framework reasons the way most economics does. Suppliers and buyers respond to the landscape: lower the ridge or reshape the terrain, and capital moves. That logic is necessary, and it explains a great deal. But it assumes that the people making decisions see the whole landscape, weigh it correctly and act on it.

Often they do not. A procurement officer may stay with a familiar supplier because switching is a personal risk with little personal reward. A manufacturer may discount a buyer's forecast because the last one was wrong. A ministry may wait for a global guideline before changing a treatment protocol, even when the evidence is clear. None of this is irrational from where each person sits. It is simply not what the model predicts, and it is often the real reason capital stays where it is.

The catalyst works in the gap between how markets are modeled and how they behave. A financial instrument is just a contract; a <Term>volume guarantee</Term> is ink on paper. What turns it into market change is an organization that understands the people and institutions in a market well enough to move them: their constraints, their incentives, their habits and whom they trust.

</Section>

<Section id="frictions" tone="slate" title="Six Frictions the Model Leaves Out" intro="Behavioral and institutional economics have names for most of what keeps real markets from behaving like the model. Each one is part of the ridge in a transitional gap, and each points to something a catalyst can do." wide>
<div class="not-prose grid md:grid-cols-2 lg:grid-cols-3 gap-4">
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">Limited Attention</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">Herbert Simon, who received the Nobel Prize in economics in 1978, argued that people and organizations have limited time, attention and information, so they settle for options that are good enough rather than searching for the best. He called this <Term>bounded rationality</Term>. A better product or price can go unconsidered simply because no one has time to evaluate it.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> does the analysis and puts the option in front of the people who decide.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">Fear of Loss</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">The psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed in 1979 that people weigh losses more heavily than equal gains; Kahneman later received the Nobel Prize in economics. A related <Term>status quo bias</Term> favors whatever is already in place. Switching suppliers or products puts a visible loss at risk for the person who signs, while the gain is spread across many others.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> lowers the personal risk of switching: technical guidance, transition plans, examples from peers.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">Coordination Without a Focal Point</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">Thomas Schelling, a Nobel laureate in economics, observed in 1960 that when people need to coordinate but cannot easily agree, they converge on whatever option seems obvious. He called it a <Term>focal point</Term>. In a coordination failure, each party waits for the others because nothing makes one outcome the obvious one.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> convenes the parties, sequences their commitments and gives them a common point to converge on.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">Quality No One Can Verify</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">George Akerlof, another Nobel laureate, showed in 1970 that when buyers cannot judge quality, good products can be driven out by poor ones; he used the market for used cars as his example. This <Term>information asymmetry</Term> is why quality assurance systems such as the World Health Organization's prequalification of medicines matter so much to new suppliers.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> helps suppliers through quality assurance and gives buyers information they can rely on.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">Contracts That Cannot Say Everything</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">No contract specifies everything that matters. Oliver Williamson, who shared the Nobel Prize in economics in 2009, studied how firms govern transactions that contracts cannot fully specify, and why long-term relationships and reputation carry so much weight. Suppliers share cost data, and buyers share honest forecasts, with partners they trust.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> is a trusted party to both sides, which is what makes cost analysis and process improvement possible.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">People Inside Organizations</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">Every buyer and supplier acts through individuals with incentives of their own: a budget cycle to meet, a career to protect, a superior to persuade. Economists call the gap between an organization's interest and its employee's a principal-agent problem. Markets move when the right individuals decide to move them.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> knows who actually decides, and what each of them needs in order to say yes.</p>
  </div>
</div>
</Section>

<Section id="what-a-catalyst-does" title="What a Catalyst Does">

In practice, catalytic work is less visible than the instrument it supports, and usually takes longer. It includes:

- **Convening and sequencing.** Bringing suppliers, buyers, regulators and funders together, and ordering their commitments so that no one has to move first alone.
- **Making information credible.** Market intelligence, demand forecasts and cost analysis that each side can rely on because they trust where it came from.
- **Brokering trust.** Acting as a neutral party that suppliers will share cost data with and buyers will believe.
- **Lowering the personal risk of change.** Technical guidance, transition plans and examples from peers, so that the individual who signs is not exposed alone.
- **Changing the default.** Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein showed how strongly defaults shape choices; Thaler later received the Nobel Prize in economics. Updating treatment guidelines, tender templates and procurement lists makes the better option the path of least resistance.
- **Adapting.** Markets change after the deal is signed. Someone has to notice when a supplier is struggling, a forecast is wrong or a buyer is wavering, and respond before the gains unwind.

The transition to TLD for HIV illustrates the point. A lower price and a volume guarantee were necessary, but countries also needed updated treatment guidelines, transition plans and confidence in supply before procurement officers would switch ([see the case](/cases/tld/)).

</Section>

<Section id="in-the-ratio" title="The Catalyst in the Ratio">

The catalyst's work is a cost, and it belongs in the denominator of the [cost-effectiveness ratio](/cost-effectiveness/). It also changes the numerator, through the probability of success and through which levers fire. Process improvement, for example, depends on suppliers sharing cost data with someone they trust. That makes the quality of the catalyst one of the larger determinants of cost-effectiveness, and one of the least measured.

It also raises a hard attribution question. The catalyst's contribution is rarely separable from the instrument it makes work, and the field has no settled way to divide credit between them ([open questions](/questions/)).

</Section>
