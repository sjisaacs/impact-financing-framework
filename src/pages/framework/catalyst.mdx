<Section id="where-the-model-ends" title="The Limits of the Simple Model">

The rest of this framework reasons the way most economics does. Suppliers and buyers respond to the landscape, so lowering the ridge or reshaping the terrain moves capital. The reasoning is necessary and explains a great deal, but in its simplest form it treats each firm, ministry or funder as a single decision-maker that sees the whole landscape and acts on it.

Real markets depart from that picture in two different ways, and the two need to be told apart.

**People act rationally, but for themselves and with incomplete information.** A procurement officer may stay with a familiar supplier because a failed switch would damage their career, while the savings from a successful one go to the program. The officer is responding sensibly to incentives that are not aligned with the organization's, which is a <Term>principal-agent problem</Term>. A manufacturer may discount a buyer's demand forecast because the last one was wrong and it cannot check the new one. A ministry may wait for a global guideline before changing a treatment protocol, because acting first exposes it to criticism if the evidence shifts. Economics has well-developed tools for these problems, in principal-agent theory and the economics of information and contracts.

**People also depart from rational choice in predictable ways.** They settle for options that are good enough, weigh losses more heavily than equal gains, and stay with whatever is already in place. Behavioral economics studies these patterns.

The two call for different responses. Misaligned incentives are addressed by changing what the individual stands to gain or lose, and missing information by supplying information people can trust. Biases are addressed by changing how choices are framed, sequenced and set as defaults. A model of fully informed, rational actors includes neither kind of problem, and each is a common reason capital stays where it is.

The catalyst deals with the difference between how markets are modeled and how they behave. A financial instrument such as a <Term>volume guarantee</Term> is a contract, and turning it into market change takes an organization that understands the people and institutions in a market well enough to influence them: their incentives, what they know, whom they trust and how they actually decide.

</Section>

<Section id="incentives-and-information" tone="slate" title="Misaligned Incentives and Incomplete Information" intro="In each of these four problems, people act sensibly given their own position, but the market as a whole stays stuck. All four are studied in the economics of information, contracts and organizations." wide>
<div class="not-prose grid md:grid-cols-2 gap-4">
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">Principal-Agent Problems</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">Every buyer and supplier acts through individuals with incentives of their own: a budget cycle to meet, a career to protect, a superior to persuade. Economists call the gap between what an organization wants and what its staff are rewarded for a principal-agent problem. The procurement officer who avoids a risky switch is acting rationally for themselves instead of for the program.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> knows who actually decides and changes what they face: shared accountability, technical backing, and recognition when a switch works.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">Information Asymmetry</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">George Akerlof, who received the Nobel Prize in economics in 2001, showed in 1970 that when buyers cannot judge quality, good products can be driven out by poor ones; he used the market for used cars as his example. Buyers who discount every new supplier are responding rationally to this <Term>information asymmetry</Term>, so quality assurance systems such as the World Health Organization's prequalification of medicines are very important to new entrants.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> helps suppliers through quality assurance and gives buyers information they can rely on.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">Incomplete Contracts</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">No contract specifies everything that matters. Oliver Williamson, who shared the Nobel Prize in economics in 2009, studied how firms govern transactions that contracts cannot fully specify, and why each side guards against being exploited by the other. A supplier that will not share its cost data with a stranger is protecting itself, but will share the data with a partner it trusts.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> is trusted by both sides, and that trust makes cost analysis and process improvement possible.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">Coordination Failures</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">In a <Term>coordination failure</Term>, each party would gladly move if the others did, so each waits. Thomas Schelling, who shared the Nobel Prize in economics in 2005, observed in 1960 that people facing such problems converge on whatever option seems obvious to everyone, which he called a <Term>focal point</Term>. Where nothing makes one outcome obvious, the market can stay stuck.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> convenes the parties, sequences their commitments and gives them a common point to converge on.</p>
  </div>
</div>
</Section>

<Section id="departures-from-rational-choice" title="Departures from Rational Choice" intro="Three findings from behavioral economics describe systematic departures from the fully rational model, which matter most where decisions are unfamiliar, complex or made under time pressure." wide>
<div class="not-prose grid md:grid-cols-3 gap-4">
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">Bounded Rationality</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">Herbert Simon, who received the Nobel Prize in economics in 1978, argued that people and organizations have limited time, attention and capacity to process information, so they settle for options that are good enough instead of searching for the best. He called this <Term>bounded rationality</Term>. A better product or price can go unconsidered because no one has had time to evaluate it.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> does the analysis and puts the option, clearly compared, in front of the people who decide.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">Loss Aversion and Status Quo Bias</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">The psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed in 1979 that people weigh a loss more heavily than a gain of the same size; Kahneman later received the Nobel Prize in economics. A related <Term>status quo bias</Term> favors whatever is already in place. Faced with a new product whose expected benefit clearly exceeds its risk, decision-makers may still hold back because the possible downside looms larger than the likely upside.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> makes the new option familiar and the downside smaller: pilots, phased transitions, evidence from peers.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">Defaults</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">The economist Richard Thaler, who received the Nobel Prize in economics in 2017, and the legal scholar Cass Sunstein showed in <em>Nudge</em> (2008) how strongly defaults shape choices: people tend to accept whatever option requires no action. Some of that pull is rational, because changing takes effort, but people also stay with defaults more than the effort explains. In procurement, the default is often last year's tender, last year's supplier and the product already on the national list.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> helps update guidelines, tender templates and essential medicines lists, so the better option becomes the default.</p>
  </div>
</div>
</Section>

<Section id="what-a-catalyst-does" tone="slate" title="What a Catalyst Does">

Catalytic work is less visible than the instrument it supports, and usually takes longer. It starts before the deal, with knowing whose decisions matter, and continues well after it.

- **Mapping the actors.** Identifying everyone whose decision or consent the change depends on, beyond the obvious supplier and buyer: national and local government, regulators and normative bodies such as the World Health Organization, distributors, private providers, health workers, civil society and patient groups. The actor who blocks a change is often not the one the deal was designed around.
- **Aligning individual incentives.** Making it safe and worthwhile for decision-makers to act in their organization's interest: technical backing, shared accountability, and credit when the change works.
- **Making information credible.** Market intelligence, demand forecasts and cost analysis that each side relies on because it trusts the source.
- **Brokering trust.** Acting as a neutral party that suppliers will share cost data with and buyers will believe.
- **Convening and sequencing.** Bringing suppliers, buyers, regulators and funders together, and ordering their commitments so that no one has to move first alone.
- **Changing frames and defaults.** Doing the comparison for busy decision-makers, phasing transitions so the possible loss feels smaller, and making the better option the path of least resistance.
- **Following through to the end user.** Much of the work of getting the product used comes after the deal: forecasting and quantifying demand, updating stock and ordering systems, training health workers, closing infrastructure gaps such as cold chain, securing domestic or donor financing for scale-up, and building awareness among the people the product is for. The cost-effectiveness ratio is calculated on outcomes delivered, so this work often determines the size of the change.
- **Adapting.** Markets change after the deal is signed. Someone has to notice when a supplier is struggling, a forecast is wrong or a buyer is wavering, and respond before the gains unwind.

In the transition to TLD for HIV, a lower price and a volume guarantee were necessary, but countries also needed updated treatment guidelines, transition plans and confidence in supply before procurement officers would switch ([see the case](/cases/tld/)).

</Section>

<Section id="in-the-ratio" tone="slate" title="The Catalyst in the Ratio">

The catalyst's work is a cost that belongs in the denominator of the [cost-effectiveness ratio](/cost-effectiveness/), and it also changes the numerator, through the probability of success and the levers used. Process improvement, for example, depends on suppliers sharing cost data with someone they trust. How well the catalyst works therefore has a large effect on cost-effectiveness, and few analyses measure it.

The catalyst also makes attribution difficult. Its contribution can rarely be separated from that of the instrument it supports, and the field has no settled way to divide credit between them ([open questions](/questions/)).

</Section>

    <h2 class="font-serif text-3xl md:text-4xl mb-6 text-center">Before You Design an Instrument</h2>
    <ol class="space-y-3 text-blue-100 list-decimal pl-6 mb-8">
      <li>Who, by name and role, must decide differently for this to work, including those beyond the supplier and the buyer?</li>
      <li>What does each of them personally stand to gain or lose?</li>
      <li>What do they know, and what would they need to see to believe it?</li>
      <li>Whom do they trust?</li>
      <li>What will they do by default, and could the better option become the default?</li>
      <li>What has to happen after the deal for the product to reach the people who need it?</li>
      <li>Who will do this work, for how long, and who will pay for it?</li>
    </ol>
