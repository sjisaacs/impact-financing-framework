<Section id="where-the-model-ends" title="Where the Model Ends">

The rest of this framework reasons the way most economics does. Suppliers and buyers respond to the landscape: lower the ridge or reshape the terrain, and capital moves. That logic is necessary, and it explains a great deal. But in its simplest form it treats each firm, ministry or funder as a single decision-maker that sees the whole landscape and acts on it.

Real markets depart from that picture in two different ways, and it matters which one is at work.

**People act rationally, but for themselves and with incomplete information.** A procurement officer may stay with a familiar supplier because a failed switch would damage their career, while the savings from a successful one go to the program. That is not a bias. It is a sensible response to incentives that are not aligned with the organization's. A manufacturer may discount a buyer's demand forecast because the last one was wrong, which is a reasonable response to information it cannot verify. A ministry may wait for a global guideline before changing a treatment protocol, because acting first exposes it to criticism if the evidence shifts. Economics has well-developed tools for these problems, in principal-agent theory and the economics of information and contracts.

**People also depart from rational choice in predictable ways.** They settle for options that are good enough, weigh losses more heavily than equal gains, and stay with whatever is already in place. This is the territory of behavioral economics.

The two call for different responses. Misaligned incentives are addressed by changing what the individual stands to gain or lose, and missing information by supplying information people can trust. Biases are addressed by changing how choices are framed, sequenced and set as defaults. Neither appears in a model of fully informed, rational actors, and both are common reasons capital stays where it is.

The catalyst works in that gap between how markets are modeled and how they behave. A financial instrument is just a contract; a <Term>volume guarantee</Term> is ink on paper. What turns it into market change is an organization that understands the people and institutions in a market well enough to move them: their incentives, what they know, whom they trust and how they actually decide.

</Section>

<Section id="incentives-and-information" tone="slate" title="Rational, but Misaligned or Uninformed" intro="Four problems in which each person acts sensibly given their own position, yet the market as a whole stays stuck. All four come from the economics of information, contracts and organizations." wide>
<div class="not-prose grid md:grid-cols-2 gap-4">
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">People Inside Organizations</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">Every buyer and supplier acts through individuals with incentives of their own: a budget cycle to meet, a career to protect, a superior to persuade. Economists call the gap between what an organization wants and what its staff are rewarded for a <Term>principal-agent problem</Term>. The procurement officer who avoids a risky switch is acting rationally for themselves, not for the program.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> knows who actually decides and changes what they face: shared accountability, technical backing, and recognition when a switch works.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">Quality No One Can Verify</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">George Akerlof, who received the Nobel Prize in economics in 2001, showed in 1970 that when buyers cannot judge quality, good products can be driven out by poor ones; he used the market for used cars as his example. Buyers who discount every new supplier are responding rationally to this <Term>information asymmetry</Term>. It is why quality assurance systems such as the World Health Organization's prequalification of medicines matter so much to new entrants.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> helps suppliers through quality assurance and gives buyers information they can rely on.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">Contracts That Cannot Say Everything</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">No contract specifies everything that matters. Oliver Williamson, who shared the Nobel Prize in economics in 2009, studied how firms govern transactions that contracts cannot fully specify, and why each side guards against being exploited by the other. A supplier that will not share its cost data with a stranger is protecting itself sensibly; it will share them with a partner it trusts.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> is a trusted party to both sides, which is what makes cost analysis and process improvement possible.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-white p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">Coordination Without a Focal Point</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">In a <Term>coordination failure</Term>, each party would gladly move if the others did, so each sensibly waits. Thomas Schelling, who shared the Nobel Prize in economics in 2005, observed in 1960 that people facing such problems converge on whatever option seems obvious to everyone, which he called a <Term>focal point</Term>. Where nothing makes one outcome obvious, the market can stay stuck.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> convenes the parties, sequences their commitments and gives them a common point to converge on.</p>
  </div>
</div>
</Section>

<Section id="departures-from-rational-choice" title="Predictable Departures from Rational Choice" intro="Three findings from behavioral economics, where people depart systematically from the fully rational model. These matter most where decisions are unfamiliar, complex or made under time pressure." wide>
<div class="not-prose grid md:grid-cols-3 gap-4">
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">Limited Attention</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">Herbert Simon, who received the Nobel Prize in economics in 1978, argued that people and organizations have limited time, attention and capacity to process information, so they settle for options that are good enough rather than searching for the best. He called this <Term>bounded rationality</Term>. A better product or price can go unconsidered simply because no one has had the time to evaluate it.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> does the analysis and puts the option, clearly compared, in front of the people who decide.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">Weighing Losses More Than Gains</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">The psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed in 1979 that people weigh a loss more heavily than a gain of the same size; Kahneman later received the Nobel Prize in economics. A related <Term>status quo bias</Term> favors whatever is already in place. Faced with a new product whose expected benefit clearly exceeds its risk, decision-makers may still hold back because the possible downside looms larger than the likely upside.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> makes the new option familiar and the downside smaller: pilots, phased transitions, evidence from peers.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 p-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-200">
    <h3 class="font-serif text-xl text-slate-900 mb-2">The Pull of Defaults</h3>
    <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-3">The economist Richard Thaler, who received the Nobel Prize in economics in 2017, and the legal scholar Cass Sunstein showed in <em>Nudge</em> (2008) how strongly defaults shape choices: people tend to accept whatever option requires no action. Part of that pull is rational, because changing takes effort; part is inertia beyond what the effort justifies. In procurement, the default is often last year's tender, last year's supplier and the product already on the national list.</p>
    <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>The catalyst</strong> helps update guidelines, tender templates and essential medicines lists, so the better option becomes the default.</p>
  </div>
</div>
</Section>

<Section id="what-a-catalyst-does" tone="slate" title="What a Catalyst Does">

In practice, catalytic work is less visible than the instrument it supports, and usually takes longer. Each part of it answers one of the problems above.

- **Aligning individual incentives.** Making it safe and worthwhile for decision-makers to act in their organization's interest: technical backing, shared accountability, and credit when the change works.
- **Making information credible.** Market intelligence, demand forecasts and cost analysis that each side can rely on, because they trust where it came from.
- **Brokering trust.** Acting as a neutral party that suppliers will share cost data with and buyers will believe.
- **Convening and sequencing.** Bringing suppliers, buyers, regulators and funders together, and ordering their commitments so that no one has to move first alone.
- **Changing frames and defaults.** Doing the comparison for busy decision-makers, phasing transitions so the possible loss feels smaller, and making the better option the path of least resistance.
- **Adapting.** Markets change after the deal is signed. Someone has to notice when a supplier is struggling, a forecast is wrong or a buyer is wavering, and respond before the gains unwind.

The transition to TLD for HIV illustrates the point. A lower price and a volume guarantee were necessary, but countries also needed updated treatment guidelines, transition plans and confidence in supply before procurement officers would switch ([see the case](/cases/tld/)).

</Section>

<Section id="in-the-ratio" tone="slate" title="The Catalyst in the Ratio">

The catalyst's work is a cost, and it belongs in the denominator of the [cost-effectiveness ratio](/cost-effectiveness/). It also changes the numerator, through the probability of success and through which levers fire. Process improvement, for example, depends on suppliers sharing cost data with someone they trust. That makes the quality of the catalyst one of the larger determinants of cost-effectiveness, and one of the least measured.

It also raises a hard attribution question. The catalyst's contribution is rarely separable from the instrument it makes work, and the field has no settled way to divide credit between them ([open questions](/questions/)).

</Section>

    <ol class="space-y-3 text-blue-100 list-decimal pl-6 mb-8">
      <li>Who, by name and role, must decide differently for this to work?</li>
      <li>What does each of them personally stand to gain or lose?</li>
      <li>What do they know, and what would they need to see to believe it?</li>
      <li>Whom do they trust?</li>
      <li>What will they do by default, and could the better option become the default?</li>
      <li>Who will do this work, for how long, and who will pay for it?</li>
    </ol>
    <div class="bg-white/10 p-5 rounded-lg text-center"><p class="font-serif text-xl italic">Never design an instrument without asking who will be the engine that powers it.</p></div>
