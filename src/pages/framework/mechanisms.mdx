<Section id="why-a-separate-layer" title="Why a Separate Layer?">

Two interventions can use the same instrument and produce different impact, because the instrument works through different mechanisms in each case. Take a <Term>volume guarantee</Term>: a promise to buy, or pay for, a minimum quantity if demand falls short. It can lower a price by letting a manufacturer produce at scale, by removing the premium it charges for uncertain demand, by both, or by neither. Knowing which mechanism is doing the work tells you whether the impact will last, whether the design is over- or under-engineered, and what is left on the table if other mechanisms are skipped.

The two pathways are exhaustive by construction. Measure price as what the buyer spends per unit of the outcome it values, such as a patient-year of viral suppression or a delivered test result. That price is the supplier's cost plus its margin, so any lasting reduction must lower cost, narrow margin, or both. Within those two pathways there are six distinct levers.

        <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>Canonical case:</strong> the Clinton Foundation's first generic antiretroviral agreements in 2003 cut a common triple-therapy pill from about $300 to under $140 per patient per year, with further reductions built in as volumes grew.         <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-2">Two effects that often travel together but behave differently. <strong class="text-slate-800">Economies of scale:</strong> larger orders and longer production runs spread fixed costs over more units, raise capacity utilization and earn better prices on inputs. Financing tools become price-reduction tools when their concessional terms are passed into the contracted price.</p>
        <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>Typical case:</strong> below-market working capital for generic manufacturers, given in exchange for pricing and supply terms.</p></div>

<Section id="durability" title="Each Lever Holds Under a Different Condition" intro="A transitional intervention succeeds only if the lever it fires survives exit. The levers differ widely in how well they do.">

<div class="table-wrap">

| Lever | What it changes | Holds after exit if |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Process improvement | How the product is made | Nearly always. The supplier keeps the know-how |
| Scale | Cost per unit at a given volume | Volume persists at the new price |
| Learning | Cost per unit given everything made so far | Largely, because experience is not lost when volume dips |
| Capital cost | Financing terms | Cheaper capital stays available after the concession ends, which is often not true |
| Competition | Number of viable suppliers | Entrants stay profitable at the lower price, which thin margins often prevent |
| Disintermediation | Layers in the value chain | The removed layers don't re-form |
| Risk transfer | The uncertainty premium | The risk actually resolved, meaning demand proved real |

</div>

Process improvement is different in kind from the rest. The other levers hold only while some condition in the market holds: volume, entrants, cheap finance, demand. A redesigned process is a permanent change in what it costs to make the product, and it survives almost anything that happens to the market afterward. Learning comes closest, for the same reason.

Competition is often less durable than it looks. A lower price can attract entrants, but it also leaves less margin, and suppliers in thin markets exit, merge or stop investing. A price that depends on a third supplier is exposed if that supplier leaves.

Risk transfer has the most conditional durability. If a guarantee is never called, demand proved real and the premium disappears for good. If it is called, demand failed and the guarantee became a payment.

</Section>

<Section id="transfers-and-incentives" tone="slate" title="Financial Transfers and Innovation Incentives" intro="Two families of intervention sit alongside the six levers rather than within them. They are widely used, often effective, and often confused with lasting price reduction.">

### Subsidies Are Not Levers

A permanent subsidy, a tax credit with no end date or a guaranteed above-market price makes a product cheaper for the buyer because someone else pays part of the price. These are <Term t="Financial Transfer">financial transfers</Term>. The supplier's cost and margin are unchanged, so no lever has fired. Risk transfer is different: it removes a premium from the supplier's margin, which is why it counts as a lever.

A permanent subsidy is the right tool for a structural gap, and should be designed and funded as one: sized to the wedge, and backed by a payer that will last.

A time-limited subsidy works differently. A <Term t="Price Buy-Down">price buy-down</Term>, for example, pays part of the price for a fixed period so that buyers can afford more volume while costs fall. It is a bridge to a lever, and it succeeds if the volume it buys brings the underlying price down, through scale, learning or new entrants, before it ends. Early solar feed-in tariffs, which paid above-market prices for a set period, worked this way.

Whether a subsidy can end depends on the market rather than on what the subsidy is called. For a technology whose costs fall with volume, it can be withdrawn on schedule. For an activity whose costs will not fall, it has to continue, and if the price returns when it stops, it was a permanent subsidy all along.

### Innovation Incentives Come First

<Term t="Priority Review Voucher">Priority review vouchers</Term>, exclusivity extensions, prizes and commitments for products that do not yet exist pull innovation forward by making research for unmet needs more attractive. They cause products to exist; once a product exists, the six levers determine the price it reaches. Innovation incentives are not a seventh lever. Designs that use them well pair them with a downstream lever, so the new product reaches a sustainable access price rather than launching as a high-price niche product.

### Two Edge Cases

Pricing per unit of outcome settles two cases that are otherwise hard to place. **Product substitution**, such as replacing efavirenz with dolutegravir, is process improvement at the level of the outcome: the same viral suppression costs less to deliver. **Restructuring the price stack**, such as moving viral load testing to an all-inclusive price per test, adopts the unit the buyer actually values and makes hidden margin contestable, so it belongs with disintermediation.

</Section>
