<Section id="why-a-separate-layer" title="Why a Separate Layer?">

Two interventions can use the same instrument and produce different impact, because the instrument works through different price-reduction mechanisms in each case. A volume guarantee can deliver scale-driven cost reduction, risk-premium reduction, both, or neither. Knowing which mechanism is doing the work tells you whether the impact will last, whether the design is over- or under-engineered, and what will be left on the table if you skip the harder mechanisms.

The two pathways are exhaustive by construction. Measure price as what the <Term>payer</Term> spends per unit of the outcome it values, such as a patient-year of viral suppression or a delivered test result. That price decomposes into cost and margin, so any durable reduction must lower cost, narrow margin, or both. Within those two pathways there are six distinct levers.

<Section id="six-levers" tone="slate" title="Six Levers" wide>
<div class="not-prose grid lg:grid-cols-2 gap-6">

    <div class="p-6 space-y-6">
      <div><div class="flex items-center gap-3 mb-2"><span class="w-8 h-8 bg-blue-100 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center text-blue-700 font-bold text-sm">1</span><div><div class="font-semibold text-slate-900">Process improvement</div><div class="text-xs text-slate-500">Industrial engineering on the production process itself.</div></div></div>
        <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-2">Restructure specialty chemical sourcing, standardize packaging, dedicate lines, change cost allocation. Unit cost falls because the process is redesigned, not because volume rose.</p>
        <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>Canonical case:</strong> the Clinton Foundation's first generic antiretroviral agreements in 2003 cut a common triple-therapy pill from about $300 to under $140 per patient per year, with further reductions built in as volumes grew.</p></div>

        <p class="text-sm text-slate-600 mb-2">When concessional debt, first-loss capital or working capital lowers what a supplier pays for money, that saving flows into production cost the same way process or scale gains do. Financing tools become price-reduction tools when their concessional terms are passed into the contracted price.</p>
        <p class="text-xs text-blue-700 bg-blue-50 p-2 rounded"><strong>Typical case:</strong> below-market working capital for generic manufacturers, given in exchange for pricing and supply terms.</p></div>

<Section id="durability" title="Each Lever Holds Under a Different Condition" intro="A transitional intervention succeeds only if the lever it fires survives exit. Each lever changes something different, so each has its own durability condition.">

| Lever | What it changes | Holds after exit if |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Process improvement | How the product is made | The supplier keeps the know-how, which is nearly always |
| Scale | Position on the cost curve | Volume persists at the new price |
| Competition | Number of viable suppliers | Entrants stay profitable at the new price |
| Disintermediation | Layers in the value chain | The removed layers don't re-form |
| Capital cost | Financing terms | Cheaper capital stays available after the concession ends, which is often not true |
| Risk transfer | The uncertainty premium | The risk actually resolved, meaning demand proved real |

Risk transfer has the most conditional durability. If a guarantee is never called, demand proved real and the premium disappears for good. If it is called, demand failed and the guarantee became a payment.

</Section>

<Section id="easy-and-hard-levers" tone="slate" title="Easy Levers and Hard Levers" intro="The six levers are not equally accessible, and the accessible ones are the least durable.">

<Fig takeaway="The durable levers are the hard ones." caption="Risk transfer and capital cost can be activated through contract design alone. Process improvement and disintermediation need industrial engineering capability, supplier trust and access to cost data.">
  <LeverMap />
</Fig>

Risk transfer and capital cost reduction can be activated through financial-instrument design alone, given <Term>concessional capital</Term>. Competition can be activated through market-structure choices like price ceilings and open expressions of interest. Process improvement requires sustained engineering work, protected access to supplier cost data and capability that financial actors do not typically have in house.

The result is a structural drift toward the cheap levers. A field that started with cost-side pioneering has narrowed toward risk transfer as the default. Risk transfer alone produces a price reduction, but a much smaller one than risk transfer combined with process work upstream, and the least durable one of the six.

<Section id="parallel-approaches" title="Transfer Payments and Innovation Incentives" intro="Two families of intervention sit alongside the six levers rather than within them. They are widely used, frequently effective, and frequently confused with mechanism-driven price reduction.">

### Transfers Are Not Levers

A buy-down, a tax credit, a feed-in tariff or a permanent above-market price makes a product cheaper for the buyer because someone else pays the difference. The supplier's cost and margin are unchanged; no lever has fired.

- A **transitional <Term>transfer</Term>** is a bridge to a lever. It buys volume until scale or competition lowers the underlying price, then ends. Early solar feed-in tariffs fit this pattern.
- A **permanent transfer** sustains a price wedge with no mechanism for the market to converge. It is the right tool for a structural gap, and should be designed and funded as one.

The boundary is empirical, not declared. The same subsidy applied to a technology on a learning curve is transitional; applied to an activity whose costs will not fall, it is permanent. A transfer is transitional only if it fires a lever.

### Innovation Incentives Come First

Priority review vouchers, exclusivity extensions, prizes and commitments for products that do not yet exist pull innovation forward by making research for unmet needs more attractive. They cause products to exist; once a product exists, the six levers determine the price it reaches. Innovation incentives are not a seventh lever. Designs that use them well pair them with a downstream lever, so the new product reaches a sustainable access price rather than launching as a high-price niche product.

### Two Edges, Resolved

Earlier versions of this framework left two questions open, and pricing per unit of outcome resolves both. **Product substitution**, such as replacing efavirenz with dolutegravir, is process improvement at the level of the outcome: the same viral suppression costs less to deliver. **Restructuring the price stack**, such as per-test pricing, adopts the unit the payer actually values and makes hidden margin contestable, so it belongs with disintermediation.

</Section>
