<Section id="every-gap-needs-a-payer" title="Every Structural Gap Needs a Payer">

A <Term t="Structural Misalignment">structural gap</Term> does not close on its own, so someone must fill it for as long as the outcome matters. Payers take a few forms: governments funding through taxes, donors and multilateral funds such as Gavi and the Global Fund, compliance markets created by regulation, such as <Term>cap-and-trade</Term>, and voluntary buyers acting from mission or reputation.

They differ most in durability. Voluntary payers are the most fragile: the traded value of the voluntary carbon market peaked at around $2 billion in 2021 and fell to $535 million in 2024, according to [Ecosystem Marketplace](https://www.forest-trends.org/?p=4656710).

</Section>

<Section id="payer-first" tone="slate" title="Decompose the Gap, Payer First">

<Fig takeaway="Diagnose the payer before the lever." caption="The structural part of a gap needs a payer for as long as the outcome matters; the transitional part can be closed by a lever and left.">
  <GapDecomposition />
</Fig>

Most gaps have both parts. Today's price splits into what end users can pay, a structural gap that a payer must fill, and a transitional gap that a lever can close. Diagnosing in that order keeps the two kinds of money apart: permanent money for the structural part, contingent money for the transitional part.

</Section>

<Fig takeaway="What to do first depends on whether a payer exists." caption="Shaded: where classic market shaping operates, inside an existing payer.">

</Fig>

Global health already has its structural payers in Gavi, the Global Fund and ministries of health. Most global health market shaping is therefore transitional work inside a market reshaped long ago: the payer exists, and the job is to lower what it pays per outcome.

Climate largely lacks such a payer, which is why its toolkit is mostly about reshaping incentives. Buyer coalitions for carbon removal such as Frontier now say explicitly that each purchase needs a line of sight to long-term demand from compliance markets, regulation or government procurement ([see the case](/cases/frontier/)).

The same logic covers fields where no payer exists yet. Community mental health care in many low- and middle-income countries has effective, low-cost interventions but no structural payer at scale, so classic market shaping has little to sit inside. Building the payer comes first.

</Section>

<Section id="value-of-savings" tone="slate" title="What a Dollar Saved Is Worth">

In a market with a structural payer, a lower price matters because of what the payer does with the money it no longer spends. If demand is unmet, savings buy more of the same outcome. If demand is met, savings fund the payer's next-best use.

Either way, the value of a market intervention depends on an estimate of the payer's marginal use of funds. That links market shaping directly to the cost-effectiveness of the payer's other spending, and it is the term most often left implicit.

</Section>

<Section id="payer-durability" title="Payers That Stop Paying">

The two kinds of gap fail in different ways. Transitional solutions fail economically: the better equilibrium did not exist, or the lever did not hold. Structural solutions fail politically or financially: the payer stops paying.

Recent examples make the second failure concrete. British Columbia, which had priced carbon since 2008, [set its consumer carbon tax to zero](https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2025FIN0014-000280) on April 1, 2025 ([see the case](/cases/bc-carbon-tax/)). The 2025 US budget law [ended federal wind and solar tax credits](https://www.pierceatwood.com/alerts/congress-phases-out-energy-tax-credits) for projects that begin construction on or after July 4, 2026 and enter service after 2027. In global health, donor retrenchment raises the same question about payers that have long seemed permanent.

What helps payers last is a question the framework poses rather than answers ([open questions](/questions/)). Candidates include earmarked revenue, visible rebates to households, pooling across many funders, and legal commitments that raise the cost of reversal.

### Exposure When a Payer Retreats

In nested markets, the two failures connect. Many transitional gains, such as low prices from scale, rest on volume financed by the structural payer. When payer-financed volume falls, scale economies can reverse and entrants can exit. The framework therefore predicts which past gains are most exposed: those whose low price depends on payer-financed volume rather than on process improvement or durable competition.

</Section>
