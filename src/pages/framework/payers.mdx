<Section id="structural-payers" title="Structural Gaps Need a Payer">

A structural gap does not close on its own. Someone must keep paying to correct it: a government through taxes, subsidies or regulation, a compliance market created by regulation, such as <Term>cap-and-trade</Term>, or companies and philanthropies that pay voluntarily, for mission or reputation. The framework calls whoever does this the <Term>payer</Term>.

Payers differ most in how long they keep paying, and voluntary payers are the least reliable. According to [Ecosystem Marketplace](https://www.forest-trends.org/?p=4656710), the traded value of the voluntary carbon market peaked at around $2 billion in 2021 and fell to $535 million in 2024.

</Section>

<Section id="buyers-for-others" tone="slate" title="Buyers Who Pay for Others">

Many of the markets that matter most for health and development have buyers who purchase on behalf of people who cannot pay: governments, donors and pooled funds such as Gavi and the Global Fund. These <Term t="Buyer">buyers</Term> are part of the market, and their purchases do not correct a market failure. Their willingness to pay reflects how much they value the outcome, and suppliers respond to their demand as they would to anyone's.

Whether people who cannot pay should be served is a question of distribution and priorities, settled by those buyers' budgets. The framework asks the narrower question of whether, given the buyers that exist, the market reaches the best outcome they can sustain. When it does not, the obstacle is usually a transitional gap, such as the high-price, low-volume trap in contraceptive implants before 2013 ([see the case](/cases/implants/)), and occasionally an externality that no buyer accounts for.

</Section>

<Section id="missing-buyers" title="Where Buyers Are Missing" intro="Classic market shaping needs a buyer, and some markets lack one.">

</Fig>

Global health already has established buyers in ministries of health, Gavi, the Global Fund and other donors. Most global health market shaping is therefore transitional work in markets where the buyer exists, aimed at lowering what that buyer pays per outcome.

Climate largely lacks a buyer for the outcomes that matter most. The gap is structural, because the benefit of avoiding or removing a ton of carbon goes to everyone and no one has a private reason to pay for it. Buyer coalitions for carbon removal such as Frontier now say explicitly that each purchase needs a line of sight to long-term demand from compliance markets, regulation or government procurement ([see the case](/cases/frontier/)).

Some markets lack a buyer for a different reason. Community mental health care in many low- and middle-income countries has effective, low-cost interventions but no one buying them at scale. The gap is one of financing and priorities, which economists would not call a market failure. Whatever the reason a buyer is missing, classic market shaping has little to work with until someone commits to buy.

</Section>

<Section id="value-of-savings" tone="slate" title="What a Dollar Saved Is Worth">

Where a government or donor buys on others' behalf, a lower price matters because of what that buyer does with the money it no longer spends. Savings buy more of the same outcome where needs are unmet and fund the buyer's next-best use where they are met. Buyers usually do some of each, so the two shares should be valued separately, and money not spent on the product is worth whatever it funds next. The [implants retrospective](/cases/implants/#a-retrospective-estimate) shows how much the answer depends on the split.

Valuing a market intervention therefore requires an estimate of what the buyer's marginal dollar achieves in its other spending. Most analyses leave this out.

</Section>

<Section id="when-payers-stop" title="When Payment Stops">

A transitional intervention fails if the better equilibrium did not exist or the lever stopped working after exit. A structural one fails when the payer stops paying, for political or financial reasons.

British Columbia, which had priced carbon since 2008, [set its consumer carbon tax to zero](https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2025FIN0014-000280) on April 1, 2025 ([see the case](/cases/bc-carbon-tax/)). The 2025 US budget law [ended federal wind and solar tax credits](https://www.pierceatwood.com/alerts/congress-phases-out-energy-tax-credits) for projects that begin construction on or after July 4, 2026 and enter service after 2027.

The framework leaves open what helps payers last ([open questions](/questions/)). Candidates include earmarked revenue, visible rebates to households, pooling across many funders, and legal commitments that raise the cost of reversal.

### Exposure When Buyers Retreat

Buyers who pay for others can retreat too, as recent cuts in donor funding for global health show. For market shaping this is a demand risk, which does not make the gap structural but matters for durability, since many past gains depend on volume those buyers financed. When that volume falls, economies of scale reverse and suppliers can exit, so gains that came through scale or competition are the most exposed. Process improvements and experience-curve learning largely survive.

</Section>
