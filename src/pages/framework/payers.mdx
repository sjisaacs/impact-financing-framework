<Section id="structural-payers" title="Structural Gaps Need a Payer">

A structural gap does not close on its own. Someone must pay to correct it for as long as the outcome matters: a government through taxes, subsidies or regulation, a compliance market created by regulation, such as <Term>cap-and-trade</Term>, or voluntary buyers acting from mission or reputation. The framework calls whoever does this the <Term>payer</Term>.

Payers differ most in durability. Voluntary payers are the most fragile: the traded value of the voluntary carbon market peaked at around $2 billion in 2021 and fell to $535 million in 2024, according to [Ecosystem Marketplace](https://www.forest-trends.org/?p=4656710).

</Section>

<Section id="buyers-for-others" tone="slate" title="Buyers Who Pay for Others">

Many of the markets that matter most for health and development have buyers who purchase on behalf of people who cannot pay: governments, donors and pooled funds such as Gavi and the Global Fund. These <Term t="Buyer">buyers</Term> are not a correction to a market failure. They are part of the market. Their willingness to pay reflects how much they value the outcome, and suppliers respond to their demand as they would to anyone's.

The distinction matters for diagnosis. Whether people who cannot pay should be served is a question of distribution and priorities, settled by those buyers' budgets. The framework asks a narrower question: given the buyers that exist, does the market reach the best outcome they can sustain? When it does not, the obstacle is usually a transitional gap, such as the high-price, low-volume trap in contraceptive implants before 2013 ([see the case](/cases/implants/)), and occasionally an externality that no buyer accounts for.

</Section>

</Fig>

Global health already has established buyers in ministries of health, Gavi, the Global Fund and other donors. Most global health market shaping is therefore transitional work in markets where the buyer exists: the job is to lower what that buyer pays per outcome.

Climate largely lacks a buyer for the outcomes that matter most, and for a structural reason: the benefit of avoiding or removing a ton of carbon goes to everyone, so no one has a private reason to pay for it. Buyer coalitions for carbon removal such as Frontier now say explicitly that each purchase needs a line of sight to long-term demand from compliance markets, regulation or government procurement ([see the case](/cases/frontier/)).

Some markets lack a buyer for a different reason. Community mental health care in many low- and middle-income countries has effective, low-cost interventions but no one buying them at scale. That is not a market failure in the economic sense; it is a gap in financing and priorities. Either way, classic market shaping has little to work with until someone commits to buy.

</Section>

<Section id="value-of-savings" tone="slate" title="What a Dollar Saved Is Worth">

Where a government or donor buys on others' behalf, a lower price matters because of what that buyer does with the money it no longer spends. If needs are unmet, savings buy more of the same outcome. If they are met, savings fund the buyer's next-best use. In practice both happen at once, so value the two shares separately; the part not spent on the product is worth what it buys next, which is rarely nothing. The [implants retrospective](/cases/implants/#a-retrospective-estimate) shows how much the answer turns on the split.

Either way, the value of a market intervention depends on an estimate of the buyer's marginal use of funds. That links market shaping directly to the cost-effectiveness of the buyer's other spending, and it is the term most often left implicit.

</Section>

<Section id="when-payers-stop" title="When Payment Stops">

The two kinds of gap fail in different ways. Transitional solutions fail economically: the better equilibrium did not exist, or the lever did not hold. Structural solutions fail politically or financially: the payer stops paying.

Recent examples make the second failure concrete. British Columbia, which had priced carbon since 2008, [set its consumer carbon tax to zero](https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2025FIN0014-000280) on April 1, 2025 ([see the case](/cases/bc-carbon-tax/)). The 2025 US budget law [ended federal wind and solar tax credits](https://www.pierceatwood.com/alerts/congress-phases-out-energy-tax-credits) for projects that begin construction on or after July 4, 2026 and enter service after 2027.

What helps payers last is a question the framework poses rather than answers ([open questions](/questions/)). Candidates include earmarked revenue, visible rebates to households, pooling across many funders, and legal commitments that raise the cost of reversal.

### Exposure When Buyers Retreat

Buyers who pay for others can retreat too, and recent cuts in donor funding for global health make the risk concrete. For market shaping this is a demand risk rather than a structural failure, but it matters for durability, because many past gains rest on volume those buyers financed. When that volume falls, economies of scale reverse and suppliers can exit. The gains most exposed are those that depend on volume, through scale or competition. Process improvements and experience-curve learning largely survive.

</Section>
