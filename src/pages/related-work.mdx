**The Market Shaping Accelerator at the University of Chicago.** Founded by Michael Kremer and Rachel Glennerster with Christopher Snyder of Dartmouth, it develops pull mechanisms such as advance market commitments and applies them beyond vaccines, including to climate. Its [launch announcement](https://marketshaping.uchicago.edu/news/uchicago-launches-market-shaping-accelerator/) and its [partnership with the Center for Global Development](https://www.cgdev.org/node/3134034) describe the program. Its work is the natural reference for the design of pull mechanisms. This framework adds a question for each mechanism: who will keep buying or paying once the commitment ends.

**The Center for Global Development.** CGD's 2005 working group report, *Making Markets for Vaccines*, set out a detailed design for the advance market commitment, and its continuing work on pull financing informs the [pneumococcal case](/cases/pneumococcal-amc/).

**Global health market shaping practice.** Gavi, Unitaid, the Global Fund, the Clinton Health Access Initiative and others have built the practice this framework draws on, including the [contraceptive implants](/cases/implants/) and [TLD](/cases/tld/) cases.

**Blended finance.** Development finance institutions have set out principles for blended concessional finance, including additionality and minimum concessionality, that closely match the precision guardrails on the [cost-effectiveness page](/cost-effectiveness/#precision-and-durability). Practitioner networks maintain detailed catalogs of blended finance instruments and transactions.

**Carbon removal procurement.** [Frontier](https://frontierclimate.com/assets/annual-update-2025.pdf) applies advance market commitment logic to durable carbon removal, and is discussed in [its case](/cases/frontier/).

**Economic foundations.** The distinction between path and destination externalities draws on three economists. The development economist Paul Rosenstein-Rodan argued in 1943 for coordinated industrialization, an idea later called the "big push." Kenneth Arrow, who received the Nobel Prize in economics in 1972, described learning by doing in 1962. Arthur Pigou, a Cambridge economist, made the case for corrective taxes in *The Economics of Welfare* (1920). [The catalyst](/framework/catalyst/) draws on two bodies of work. The economics of information, contracts and organizations explains problems in which people act rationally but with misaligned incentives or incomplete information. It includes principal-agent theory and the work of three Nobel laureates in economics: George Akerlof, who analyzed information asymmetry in his 1970 paper on the "market for lemons" (Nobel 2001); Oliver Williamson, who studied how firms govern what contracts cannot specify (Nobel 2009); and Thomas Schelling, who described focal points in 1960 (Nobel 2005). Behavioral economics studies predictable departures from rational choice. The catalyst page draws on Herbert Simon's work on bounded rationality (Nobel 1978); on the psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, who showed in 1979 that people weigh a loss more heavily than a gain of the same size (Kahneman received the Nobel Prize in economics in 2002); and on the economist Richard Thaler (Nobel 2017) and the legal scholar Cass Sunstein, whose 2008 book *Nudge* showed how strongly defaults shape choices.
